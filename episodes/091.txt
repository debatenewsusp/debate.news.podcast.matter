Episode 91. State Capitalism and Anti-Globalization.
Two parts today: the case against state capitalism, and an analysis of the anti-globalization backlash.

First, state capitalism means governments steering markets through state-owned firms and industrial policy, a model China now exports.

It threatens the world economy in two ways. It triggers tit-for-tat retaliation, even among allies: Europe attacked America's Inflation Reduction Act as unfair, and with the WTO weakened, such disputes are harder to settle. And it breeds inefficiency: state firms enable rent-seeking and corruption, soft budgets reward mismanagement, as in China's bloated state sector and its crackdown on entrepreneurs like Jack Ma, and governments pick losers, like the solar company Solyndra under Obama.

It threatens the U.S. specifically. American firms struggle against subsidized state giants like Sinopec, Petrobras and Aramco, and sovereign wealth funds are eroding Wall Street's lending dominance. The U.S. faces a dilemma: lose, or copy their tactics. State capitalism also undermines U.S.-led institutions like the IMF, offers authoritarian capitalism as an alternative, and lets China use Belt and Road loans for political leverage. With the IMF and WTO paralyzed by great power rivalry, the U.S. is fighting state capitalism with its own, through tariffs, the CHIPS Act and the IRA, which paradoxically increases state intervention worldwide and strains allies.

Second, the anti-globalization backlash. The signs are everywhere: the U.S.-China trade war, Trump abandoning the Trans-Pacific Partnership, Brexit, and populists from Syriza to Bolsonaro attacking a corporate global order. Clinton once boasted of opening world markets; Trump and later Biden blamed trade deals for hollowing out the middle class.

The balanced view: nineties liberals were right about trade driving growth but underestimated its social, cultural and political costs; today's critics are right about inequality and job losses but have no clearly better alternative.

But maybe governments are not simply exaggerating globalization's costs, they are redefining priorities. The world changed: the Great Recession, the eurozone crisis, technological upheaval and rivalry with Russia and China. The CHIPS Act is not anti-trade confusion, it is a response to the chips race and supply chain risk that nineties debates never imagined.

And the backlash is two-faced. In the nineties, rhetoric matched policy: Clinton backed NAFTA and signed it. Today, governments loudly criticize globalization but quietly keep it. Illiberal moves often serve liberal goals: some Brexiteers wanted freer global trade, and Trump and Biden used tariffs to push China toward fairer practices. And much of it is just talk: Eurosceptic parties grow while EU integration continues, and U.S. protectionism targets only strategic sectors like chips and electric vehicles. As economist Dani Rodrik puts it, globalization is not collapsing, it is being reshaped.

That was State Capitalism and Anti-Globalization. Next episode: Competitive Authoritarianism.
