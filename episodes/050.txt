Episode 50. Indigenous Oppression.
Today: land, access to services, culture, and poverty.

First, land. It is the basis of indigenous economies, through farming, grazing and water. It is tied to culture, through traditional foods and practices, like the Gunditjmara eel traps in Australia, and to traditional medicine. And it underpins self-determination: self-government is nearly impossible without recognized land rights.

Those rights are under attack from agribusiness and logging in the Amazon, oil extraction like Canada's tar sands, pollution like oil spills on Ecuador's rivers, governments, as when Nigeria executed Ogoni activist Ken Saro-Wiwa, and organized crime, which has murdered land defenders in Mexico. The colonial Doctrine of Discovery treated indigenous land as empty, pushing communities onto poor, remote land; India's Adivasi have been displaced by state coal mining. Displacement fuels conflict, like land disputes with palm oil firms in Indonesia, and devastates mental health: displaced Guarani-Kaiowá in Brazil have suicide rates many times the national average.

Second, public services. Communities often live far from hospitals and universities, with poor roads and internet. Language barriers and culturally insensitive providers deter people; doctors sometimes wrongly assume indigenous patients are addicts and withhold medication. Lower incomes make insurance unaffordable, reserves often lack trained medical staff, and memories of colonization and residential schools make people rightly wary of state institutions and keep enrollment in higher education low.

Third, culture. Forced assimilation through residential schools in Canada, the U.S. and Australia stripped children of language, dress and identity. Outright violence has ranged from Guatemala's genocide against the Maya to Fujimori's forced sterilization of hundreds of thousands of indigenous women in Peru. The result is intergenerational trauma and internalized oppression, linked to violence: a large majority of indigenous women in the U.S. and Canada experience physical or sexual violence. Globalization adds assimilation pressure and commodifies indigenous culture.

Fourth, poverty. Indigenous people are about six percent of the world's population but nearly a fifth of the extreme poor. They face hiring and wage discrimination, lower literacy, housing instability and lack of ID and transport. Their lands are exploited by extractive industries and settlers, many live in remote areas with few jobs, infrastructure denies clean water and sanitation, and climate change threatens land-dependent livelihoods.
