Episode 12. Drone Strikes.
Today: background on U.S. drone strikes, the case for them, and the case against.

First, background. Drone use began under Bush and expanded sharply under Obama. Since 9/11 the U.S. has carried out tens of thousands of strikes, many against high-value targets like Soleimani and Zawahiri. Most are run by the CIA with little congressional oversight, and data is murky: militaries often count any military-age male as a combatant, and access to strike sites is restricted. Drone warfare has now spread to Ukraine, Azerbaijan, Yemen and beyond.

Now, the case for drones.

They are versatile. They are hard to detect, reach difficult terrain, and can loiter for over fourteen hours waiting for the right moment, while a fighter jet stays up for about four. They work where boots on the ground are impossible, like Pakistan's tribal regions, where the government lacked control, or Yemen, where local officials might tip off militants about raids.

They may actually reduce civilian harm. Operators are not in danger, so they act less on fear. Strikes can be called off in real time as new information comes in. And drones carry smaller munitions than conventional missiles, which need big blast radiuses to make up for inaccuracy.

They hurt terrorist groups. Drones have killed leaders of the Pakistani Taliban, al-Qaeda in the Arabian Peninsula and al-Shabaab. Removing skilled operatives breaks cohesion. Fear of drones stops militants from communicating digitally, gathering in groups or staying in one place, which disrupts planning and pushes fragmented groups toward negotiation.

Now, the case against.

Civilian casualties are huge, with estimates around twenty-two thousand killed, including the 2008 wedding party strike in Afghanistan. Operators act with impunity from far away. Militants live with families, and increasingly in dense cities. Intelligence is often faulty. Double-tap strikes hit rescuers and medics, and signature strikes target behavior that merely looks suspicious.

They undermine local governments, which look weak when a foreign power violates their sovereignty. That makes terrorists less willing to negotiate and lets them pose as the real authority.

They boost recruitment. Strikes are deeply unpopular in places like Pakistan and Somalia, they drive revenge, and groups like al-Qaeda use them in propaganda. Fighters from broken cells just join other groups.

Finally, cheap drones fuel an arms race, make it easy to drift into wars of little interest, and amount to extrajudicial killing under international law.
