Episode 132. ICC.
Today: how the International Criminal Court works, its key principles, landmark cases and criticisms.

First, the basics. The ICC prosecutes individuals for genocide, crimes against humanity, war crimes and the crime of aggression. It was created by the Rome Statute, adopted in 1998 and in force since 2002, and about a hundred and twenty-five countries are members. Major exceptions include the U.S., China, India and Russia. Do not confuse it with the International Court of Justice, the UN court that settles disputes between states. Its roots are the Nuremberg and Tokyo tribunals after World War Two and the UN tribunals for Yugoslavia and Rwanda in the nineties. It has a chief prosecutor and eighteen judges in three chambers, no more than one per country.

Second, U.S. hostility. The Bush administration refused to join and passed a law nicknamed the Hague Invasion Act, authorizing any means necessary, even force, to free Americans held by the court. Trump later sanctioned ICC officials.

Third, three limits. Non-retroactivity: it can only try crimes committed after 2002. Limited jurisdiction: crimes must occur on a member state's territory or by its nationals, unless the UN Security Council refers a case. And complementarity, the most important: the ICC acts only when a state is unable or unwilling to genuinely investigate and prosecute. If a national court runs a genuine trial, even ending in acquittal, the ICC cannot step in.

Fourth, cases. Its first conviction, in 2012, was Thomas Lubanga, a Congolese militia leader, for using child soldiers, with about ten million dollars in reparations. The trial was criticized because the prosecutor withheld possibly exculpatory evidence, and the narrow charges limited which victims got reparations. In 2014, another Congolese militia leader, Germain Katanga, was convicted, while his co-accused was acquitted, a decision criticized for setting too high a burden of proof.

Fifth, criticism. For years almost every indictment targeted Africans, fueling accusations of bias and anger in the African Union. Burundi left in 2017, while under investigation, and the Philippines left in 2019 after the court examined Duterte's drug war.

Since the file was written, the court has broadened its targets: arrest warrants for Vladimir Putin in 2023 and Benjamin Netanyahu in 2024, and former Philippine president Rodrigo Duterte was arrested and sent to The Hague in 2025. In response, the second Trump administration sanctioned the court's prosecutor and judges.
