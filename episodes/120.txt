Episode 120. European Union.
A toolkit episode today: how the EU's institutions work, what powers it has, how it votes, and how countries join.

First, the institutions. The European Council is the collective presidency: national leaders meeting a few times a year to set the agenda, settle disputes and amend treaties, and it nominates the Commission president. The European Commission is the executive: one commissioner per country, each covering a policy area and representing the EU rather than their nation. It alone proposes laws, drafts the budget and enforces EU law. The European Parliament is directly elected every five years by proportional representation, with seats roughly proportional to population, and members sit by political group, not nationality. It cannot propose laws but votes on them and approves the Commission. The Council of the EU, made of national ministers by topic, is effectively the upper house, passing laws jointly with Parliament. The Court of Justice ensures EU law is applied uniformly, settles disputes between governments and institutions, and answers questions from national courts, with a General Court below it. And the European Central Bank sets monetary policy for euro countries, with a mandate focused on price stability and protected independence. Since Brexit, the EU has twenty-seven members, most of them using the euro.

Second, competences. The EU can only act if three principles hold: conferral, meaning only powers granted by the treaties; proportionality, doing no more than needed; and subsidiarity, acting only where it can do better than national governments. It has exclusive power over the customs union, competition rules, monetary policy for the eurozone, trade deals and fisheries conservation. It shares power with member states on the single market, energy, transport, agriculture, the environment, consumer protection, migration and justice. And it only supports national action in areas like health, education, culture, tourism and sport.

Third, voting. A qualified majority requires fifty-five percent of member states representing sixty-five percent of the EU population. Unanimity, meaning any country can veto, applies to foreign and security policy, new members, taxation, the budget and some justice matters. That is how Hungary has been able to block aid packages for Ukraine. Under its common defense policy, the EU has run dozens of civilian and military missions since 2003, starting in Bosnia and North Macedonia.

Fourth, enlargement. Candidates must meet the Copenhagen criteria: stable democracy with free elections, free press, independent courts and minority rights, a working market economy, and acceptance of all EU law. The process has three stages: candidate status, long negotiations to adopt EU law, and final approval by every member state. Candidates include Albania, North Macedonia, Montenegro, Serbia and Turkey, whose talks have long stalled, and since Russia's invasion, Ukraine and Moldova, plus Bosnia and Georgia. For the Western Balkans, the EU uses Stabilization and Association Agreements: trade deals with nearly duty-free access that also require aligning with EU law, step by step toward membership.
