Episode 15. Censorship.
Two sides today: corporate censorship is good, and corporate censorship is bad.

First, corporate censorship is good.

Companies only censor in rare, clear-cut cases. Censorship risks driving users away and attracting backlash for attacking free speech, so profit discourages overreach. When a target is genuinely popular, their supporters push back as hard as activists push in. Employees disagree internally, so platforms act only when there is broad consensus. And moderation tools keep getting better at separating hate from legitimate speech.

Removing harmful content works. Less exposure means fewer people fall into the radicalization pipeline. The threat of bans makes radical speakers moderate themselves. Leaving content up lends it credibility, while bans break cults of personality. Hateful content also silences minorities, and it spirals as people share it.

On weighing: harmful speakers are artificially persuasive, because pseudoscience is easy to sell and hard to refute, and they are often charismatic. The benefit of protecting targeted groups outweighs the cost of sometimes removing borderline speech. Finally, platforms own what they built, and choosing not to act is itself a choice that makes them complicit.

Now, corporate censorship is bad.

Companies go too far. As bans become normal, the bar for bad speech keeps falling. Profit-driven firms remove criticism of themselves and cave to loud, vocal minorities to end outcry quickly. Small organized groups care more than the disengaged majority, and powerful, well-connected groups are best placed to demand removals.

There is a chilling effect: even if mostly bad speech is removed, ordinary people fear being cancelled and stay quiet.

And banning hateful content backfires. Conspiracy theories claim elites silence the truth, so bans confirm them. Banned speakers become free speech martyrs. The Streisand effect draws more attention. Banned users move to fringe platforms with no counter-voices, where radicalization gets worse. Airing bad views lets them be challenged openly instead of simmering without a release valve.

Last, platforms are today's public square, so they should meet the same free speech standards as the state, and tech billionaires should not control democratic discourse.
