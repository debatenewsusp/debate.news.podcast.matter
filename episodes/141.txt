Episode 141. Thailand.
Today: Thailand's cycle of coups, the Shinawatra saga and its political deadlock.

First, history. A bloodless revolution in 1932 ended absolute monarchy. After military coups from 1947, Thailand became a close U.S. ally during the Cold War, hosting American operations in the Vietnam War. Thailand has had around a dozen successful coups. A student uprising in 1973 brought brief democracy, ended by the 1976 massacre of students at Thammasat University and another coup. Prem Tinsulanonda brought stability and growth in the eighties, but a 1991 coup led to bloody crackdowns in Black May 1992. A 1997 constitution created a fully elected parliament.

Second, Thaksin. In 2001, telecom tycoon Thaksin Shinawatra won with his Thai Rak Thai party. He was hugely popular with the rural poor for universal healthcare at thirty baht a visit, debt relief and loans for farmers, village enterprise schemes and infrastructure. But he ruled like a CEO, curbing the press and courts, and his drug war killed over two thousand five hundred people extrajudicially, while he cracked down hard on the Muslim insurgency in the south. In 2006, his family sold its telecom company to Singapore's state investor for about one point nine billion dollars tax-free, sparking corruption protests in Bangkok. The military ousted him that September, and he went into exile, later convicted in absentia. His allies kept winning elections under new party names, now Pheu Thai, and the country was paralyzed by street battles between royalist yellow shirts and pro-Thaksin red shirts, until another coup in 2014 under General Prayut Chan-o-cha.

Third, the deadlock. Under a military-drafted constitution, Prayut stayed prime minister after the 2019 election. In 2023, the progressive Move Forward party, popular with young voters and calling for reform of the lèse-majesté law that criminalizes criticism of the monarchy, came first. But the military-appointed Senate blocked its leader from becoming prime minister. Pheu Thai then allied with pro-military parties to make Srettha Thavisin prime minister, and Thaksin returned from exile, served his sentence mostly in a hospital, and soon received a royal reduction of his term.

Fourth, the economy. Thailand is Southeast Asia's second-largest economy, built on car and electronics exports and tourism. Shortages of skilled labor hold it back, but diversification away from China could help, and it is negotiating a trade deal with the EU. Chinese firms invest heavily in its Eastern Economic Corridor. Political instability remains the main risk.

Fifth, foreign policy. Western governments dislike Thailand's democratic backsliding but avoid punishing it for fear of pushing it toward China, and Bangkok has engaged closely with Myanmar's junta.

Since the file was written: Move Forward was dissolved by the courts in 2024 and relaunched as the People's Party. Courts removed Srettha in 2024 and then Thaksin's daughter Paetongtarn Shinawatra in 2025, after a leaked call with Cambodia's Hun Sen during a deadly border conflict. Anutin Charnvirakul became prime minister, and Thaksin was sent back to prison to serve his sentence.

That was Thailand. Next episode: Indonesia.
