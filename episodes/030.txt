Episode 30. Proselytization.
Today: context on missionary faiths and poverty, whether proselytizing is done well, and whether it is good or bad.

First, context. Islam's principle of da'wah, inviting people to the faith, and Catholic missionary tradition both encourage spreading religion. Buddhism, Hinduism, Judaism and Sikhism largely do not. Poorer places tend to be more religious, since development brings secular education and less reliance on religious groups, while hardship drives people toward faith for comfort.

Second, is proselytizing done well? Yes: aggressive tactics alienate people, scripture emphasizes compassion, lasting conversion requires genuine belief, scrutiny from governments and rights groups pushes moderation, missionaries want fulfilling interactions, and heavy-handed groups can be banned, as some North African states have done. No: missionaries often feel spiritually superior; religion is so tied to identity that changing it may require pressure, bribes or false promises; the groups most eager to proselytize tend to be the most zealous; foreign missionaries lack cultural context; and converting a critical mass at once is hard without coercive tactics.

Third, the case for proselytization. It reduces poverty, because religions compete for converts with schools, wells, clinics and aid. Groups like Catholic Relief Services spend billions, and after the tsunami in Indonesia, missionaries provided medicine and shelter. It pushes local churches to improve their own charity and modernize conservative teachings. It is a protected right under the Universal Declaration of Human Rights, grounded in free speech, freedom of religion, since many faiths require it, and identity. And it can bring meaning and community to people who have lost faith.

Fourth, the case against. It is exploitative: missionaries target desperate communities, tying food or medicine to conversion is close to blackmail, it happens in schools that shape children, and it can rely on deception. It is moral violence: it tells people their entire way of life leads to damnation, and because claims about God cannot be tested, it is not normal persuasion, especially when wealthy missionaries face poor communities. It enables cultural imperialism, letting powerful religions erase indigenous faiths. And it divides communities: missionaries are seen as agents of colonialism, sparking violence like attacks on Christian missionaries in India, and conversions split communities that need unity to organize.
