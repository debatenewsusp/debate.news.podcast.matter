Episode 22. Federalization.
Today: the case against federalism and decentralization, especially in developing countries.

First, decentralization worsens governance.

Local governments are more corrupt. Local officials earn less, local media is weaker, and local anti-corruption agencies are underfunded. A central government is easier for journalists and international watchdogs to monitor. Turnout in local elections is also much lower, so results reflect the popular will less.

Local governments make worse policy. Expertise gets spread thin across many regions instead of concentrated in one capable bureaucracy. Regions race to the bottom, cutting wages and labor protections to attract business. Corporations find it easier and less risky to bribe local officials. And local governments face fewer checks and balances. Central governments, because people distrust them, are bound by courts, constitutional limits and spending caps, while regional rulers, especially in ethnically homogeneous areas, can crack down on media and businesses they dislike.

Second, decentralization stunts development.

Development needs coordination: national railways, common business standards and strong institutions. Getting six provinces to agree on one project is close to impossible; one central government can just decide. Foreign investors also prefer a single authority to dozens of different permits, codes and regulators, so decentralization scares capital away. And inequality grows, because rich regions keep their tax revenues while poor regions fall further behind. Central redistribution lifts the poorest areas.

Third, decentralization deepens ethnic divides.

Many post-colonial countries have groups concentrated in particular regions. Regional parties then win by appealing to the local ethnic majority with nationalist, inflammatory messages. National politics forces parties to build multi-ethnic coalitions, which rewards moderation. Minorities living inside another group's region, like Christians in Muslim-majority parts of Nigeria or Serbs in Croatia, become easy scapegoats under regional rule, while national politics offers them more allies and more parties to turn to.
