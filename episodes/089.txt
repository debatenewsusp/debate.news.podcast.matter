Episode 89. Mercenaries.
Today: the case for private military companies, in three arguments.

Argument one: states have the right to use them. War is regrettable but sometimes just, in self-defense, defending allies or under the responsibility to protect. Because states want wars to end quickly and because the right to wage war flows from sovereignty, states get wide latitude in tactics, within limits like never targeting civilians or torturing prisoners. Just as states outsource weapons production and intelligence, they can outsource fighting.

Argument two: mercenaries may behave better than national soldiers. Consider why war crimes happen. The chaos of battle overwhelms judgment, but private firms hire battle-hardened veterans; Blackwater was founded by a former Navy SEAL, while most national soldiers have never seen combat. Military training is nationalist socialization that dehumanizes the enemy, but mercenaries fight for pay, so enemies are targets, not vermin. Soldiers morally disengage through group loyalty and sanitized language, while mercenaries, fighting for themselves, may think twice.

Reputation keeps them in line. Phones and satellites document abuses fast, and contracts depend on avoiding scandal: after Blackwater guards killed seventeen civilians in Baghdad's Nisour Square in 2007, the firm lost its Iraqi license and U.S. work, whereas a massacre by U.S. Marines at Haditha drew far less consequence. Mercenaries can be sued, as with contractors at Abu Ghraib, are hired for specific missions that are easy to monitor, compete with many rivals, and increasingly face regulation and industry codes. Groups like Russia's Wagner have created a legitimacy crisis, so reputable firms must prove they follow the law, and liberal states need legitimate contractors to credibly condemn illegitimate ones.

Argument three: they have unique advantages. Yes, mercenaries prop up dictators in parts of Africa and fight in Yemen and Syria, but regimes willing to do that would use their own forces anyway. Private firms are experienced and well-equipped, often staffed by special forces veterans; in 2015, the South African firm STTEP helped push Boko Haram out of the Sambisa Forest within weeks after years of Nigerian failure. They supplement underfunded, bureaucratic militaries cheaply, which matters for new democracies; Sierra Leone hired Executive Outcomes to fight the brutal RUF rebels in the nineties. They let states fight without risking their own soldiers, filling gaps when armies withdraw and fighting piracy in the Gulf of Aden. They often hire locals who know the terrain and culture. They protect refugee camps and pipelines, train local armies and gather intelligence. And they may even reduce conflict: small, wealthy states can buy deterrence, and countries can reduce dependence on former colonial powers, as Papua New Guinea tried to do with Australia.
