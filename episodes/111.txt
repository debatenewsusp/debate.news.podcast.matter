Episode 111. FARC Insurgency.
Today: Colombia's background, why FARC rose, the other armed groups, the drug war, and the 2016 peace deal.

First, Colombia. A former Spanish colony, mostly Catholic and mestizo, with deep inequality: the top tenth earn about forty percent of national income, and rural poverty is far higher than urban. Violence and the search for jobs drove mass migration to cities.

Second, history. After independence under Bolívar, Liberals and Conservatives dominated politics. The 1948 assassination of Liberal leader Jorge Eliécer Gaitán sparked a decade of civil war known as La Violencia. Afterward, the two parties shared power in the National Front, alternating the presidency, while the U.S., through Kennedy's Alliance for Progress, pushed Colombia to crush leftist militias.

Third, why FARC rose. Colombia's mineral and land wealth stayed with elite families of Spanish descent. A U.S.-advised development push in the sixties evicted hundreds of thousands of small farming families, as commercial agriculture took most arable land. The National Front shut communists out of politics. And in 1964 the army attacked the communist enclave of Marquetalia; about four dozen fighters led by Manuel Marulanda escaped into the mountains and founded FARC, a Marxist-Leninist guerrilla army.

Fourth, its growth. FARC funded itself through cocaine and kidnapping, with tens of thousands of kidnappings over the years, and by the eighties attacked army units. A 1984 ceasefire let it create a political party, the Patriotic Union, but hundreds of its members were assassinated. In the nineties FARC retreated to rural strongholds, keeping peasant support. Other groups included the urban M-19 and the student-led ELN.

Fifth, right-wing paramilitaries, encouraged early on by U.S. counterinsurgency advice, rose to fight the guerrillas. They caused most civilian deaths and controlled far more of the cocaine trade than FARC. Western companies were implicated: Chiquita paid the paramilitary AUC even after the U.S. listed it as a terrorist group, and other firms were accused of hiring paramilitaries against unions and saboteurs.

Sixth, the drug war. U.S. aid poured in, but it helped FARC: as cartels like Medellín were destroyed, FARC moved into their territory, and aerial spraying of coca crops pushed farmers toward the guerrillas.

Finally, peace. In 2016, under President Juan Manuel Santos, the government and FARC leader Timochenko agreed a deal: FARC would disarm to the UN, release child soldiers and explain its crimes, in exchange for amnesty, reintegration support and guaranteed seats in Congress. Voters narrowly rejected it in a referendum, many feeling amnesty denied justice. A revised deal was ratified by Congress weeks later, FARC handed over thousands of weapons, and Santos won the Nobel Peace Prize.

That was FARC Insurgency. Next episode: Venezuela.
