Episode 85. Development Aid.
Today: how aid works, the case against it, and the case for it.

First, the basics. Aid is bilateral, from one country to another, or multilateral, through bodies like the World Bank. Most comes from governments, as grants or cheap loans. In the 2010s the biggest recipients included Afghanistan, India and Ethiopia, and the biggest donors were the U.S., Germany, the U.K., France and Japan, while Norway and Sweden gave the most relative to their economies. USAID, America's main aid agency when the file was written, worked in over a hundred countries on food, health, water and governance; it has since been largely dismantled and folded into the State Department.

Second, against aid. It creates dependence, so governments put off growth policies, and when donors cut back, often during recessions, economies crash and populists exploit resentment. It reduces pressure to fight corruption, since governments do not need to convince bond markets or taxpayers. Free aid undercuts local producers, like farmers. Money gets diverted through patronage and complex projects. Donors misunderstand local conditions, sometimes through prejudice and distance. Inflows strengthen the currency and hurt exports, the Dutch disease. Aid often comes with austerity conditions, and it can be stolen by armed groups. Politically, it makes recipients afraid to cross donors, for example by nationalizing industries or raising tariffs, and it undermines faith in local governments while Western involvement fuels resentment rooted in colonialism.

Third, for aid. The alternative is China. Poor countries still need financing, and the Belt and Road Initiative is ready to provide it, with critics warning of debt traps and seized collateral, plus China's poor human rights record. Aid also does real good: it builds schools that raise skills and attract investment, health systems that respond to epidemics, and roads that connect rural areas to markets. And if countries will seek influence abroad anyway, aid is a far better tool than military interventions, sanctions or forced austerity.
