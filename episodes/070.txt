Episode 70. Broken Windows Policing.
Today: the theory, how New York applied it, and the criticisms.

First, the theory. Broken windows theory comes from a 1982 Atlantic article by James Wilson and George Kelling: if one broken window is left unrepaired, the rest will soon be broken, because disorder signals that nobody is in control. So, the argument goes, preventing serious crime means cracking down on small offenses like vandalism, fare dodging, public drinking and graffiti. Disorder causes crime in three ways. It nudges people toward breaking rules by making it seem normal, the way people litter in stadiums but never in museums. It creates fear, so residents withdraw from public spaces, weakening the community ties that keep order. And it attracts criminals and gangs who see a low risk of getting caught.

Broken windows policing means citations and arrests for minor offenses, plus fixing derelict buildings. Supporters add that it deters crime, restores community bonds, and catches serious offenders, since stops lead to warrant checks, intelligence and seized guns.

Second, New York. Under Mayor Giuliani from 1993, police commissioner Bill Bratton went all in: subway fare-evasion sweeps, arrests of squeegee men, a war on graffiti, data-driven patrols through CompStat, and later millions of stop-and-frisk encounters. Crime fell dramatically, with murders down around sixty percent in four years. But other factors explain much of it: falling unemployment, the decline of crack cocaine, earlier policing reforms and falling crime nationwide. The NYPD's own 2015 review found no clear link between misdemeanor enforcement and felony crime, and crime kept falling after that enforcement dropped.

Third, the criticisms. It empowers racism: Black and Latino New Yorkers made up the vast majority of those stopped, and almost nine in ten were innocent, because vague criteria like furtive movements invite bias. It destroys trust, as arbitrary, aggressive stops alienate communities; the Justice Department condemned Baltimore's zero-tolerance policing for unconstitutional stops. Jailing or fining minor offenders traps people in cycles of crime, clogs courts and spreads police thin. It criminalizes poverty, punishing homeless people for being poor, with young officers chasing quotas in communities they do not understand. And the link between disorder and fear is weak: fear of crime does not track actual crime, and heavy policing can increase fear rather than reduce it.
