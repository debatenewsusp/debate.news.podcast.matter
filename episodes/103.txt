Episode 103. Post Conflict Societies.
Today: why civil wars happen, key case studies, and the two main paths to justice afterward: prosecutions and truth commissions.

First, context. Since 1945, wars between states have become rare and short, while civil wars are far more common, last around six years on average and kill several times more people.

Second, why civil wars happen. They need a motive and an opportunity: money, weapons, recruits and remote terrain. Grievance: poverty means people have little to lose, as in Mobutu's Zaire, where kleptocracy left half the population malnourished before war broke out. Ethnicity: divisions rarely cause war on their own, but elites politicize them through unequal treatment, scapegoating and propaganda. Greed: rebels profit from oil fields, diamond mines and smuggling, and resources are easy to seize and cannot relocate. Afterward, liberation movements often become new oppressors, because the secrecy, obedience and violence needed to survive an insurgency live on, and heroes are hard to hold accountable. Many countries fall into a conflict trap, like the Philippines' Mindanao or Côte d'Ivoire.

Third, case studies. Rwanda: Belgian colonizers invented rigid Hutu and Tutsi categories on identity cards, and later Hutu elites used quotas, propaganda radio calling Tutsis cockroaches and community meetings to incite civilians; after President Habyarimana's plane was shot down in 1994, around seven hundred thousand people were killed in a hundred days. Angola and Biafra show greed: UNITA earned billions from blood diamonds, and Niger Delta oil drove both Biafra's secession and Nigeria's crackdown. Colombia's FARC began as a Marxist peasant movement and became a cocaine-funded army. Mugabe and the ANC show liberators turned abusers: Mugabe's massacres and evictions, and the ANC's corruption and the 2012 Marikana mine massacre. Yugoslavia: Tito held it together until his death, then economic crisis and Milošević's nationalism led to wars that killed around two hundred thousand, and the UN tribunal, the ICTY, convicted ninety-one people.

Fourth, prosecutions. Benefits: catharsis for victims, a clear break from the old regime, deterrence, public evidence that discredits propaganda, removing the leaders of violent movements, assigning individual rather than collective guilt, and preventing vigilante revenge. Problems: powerful veto players shape the rules to protect themselves; victor's justice, since winners never prosecute their own crimes, as with Dresden or the firebombing of Tokyo; corruption and political pressure, as when U.S. influence shaped Saddam Hussein's trial; threats against judges; and rushed show trials, like Ceaușescu's execution within days. Capacity is weak: courthouses are destroyed, lawyers flee, as in Rwanda, and communities distrust the courts. Top leaders are hardest to convict because they command from afar and destroy records, though documents convicted Guatemala's Ríos Montt of genocide. International courts bring expertise and impartiality but feel foreign and neo-colonial, sit far away in The Hague or Arusha, take decades, and judges may face their own pressures.

Fifth, truth and reconciliation commissions. Truth discredits rumors, validates victims and builds a shared memory. Commissions often trade amnesty for testimony. The case for them: prosecution is often impossible after negotiated peace, as in South Africa; commissions can feed later trials, as Argentina's Never Again report did for the 1985 trial of the juntas; they gather more information, since amnesty encourages confessions up the chain of command; they write history rather than binary verdicts; they center victims instead of hostile cross-examination; and they handle moral gray zones like child soldiers.

The problems: bias and political obstruction, as in Kosovo; no subpoena powers and small budgets; narrow mandates, like Chile's commission covering only torture that caused death, while Liberia's powerful commission implicated the president herself and was then curtailed; lost records and traumatized witnesses; retraumatizing victims while giving perpetrators a platform; recommendations governments ignore; and sidelining sexual violence against women.
