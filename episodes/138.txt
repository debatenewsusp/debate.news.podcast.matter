Episode 138. Cambodia.
Today: Cambodia's tragic history, the Hun family's rule, its alignment with China, and its economy.

First, history. Cambodia won independence from France in 1953 under Norodom Sihanouk, who tried to stay neutral. Expecting communist victories next door, he cut off U.S. aid, aligned with China and let North Vietnamese forces use eastern Cambodia and supply routes, prompting secret U.S. bombing from 1969. In 1970, Lon Nol overthrew him and sided with the U.S., while Sihanouk backed Pol Pot's communist Khmer Rouge. Massive American carpet bombing helped discredit the government, and in 1975 the Khmer Rouge took Phnom Penh.

Second, the genocide. Pol Pot's regime emptied the cities, forced people into labor camps and carried out mass executions, killing around two million people, about a quarter of the population. At the S-21 prison, thousands were tortured and killed. Khmer Rouge raids into Vietnam, including a massacre of Vietnamese villagers in 1978, led Vietnam to invade in December 1978 and topple the regime in early 1979. Vietnam installed a government that became the Cambodian People's Party, and Hun Sen rose to lead it. Fighting with the Khmer Rouge continued until the 1991 Paris Peace Agreements. A UN-backed tribunal later spent nineteen years and about three hundred million dollars to convict just three leaders.

Third, politics. The CPP has ruled since 1979. Hun Sen was prime minister from 1985 until 2023, when his son Hun Manet succeeded him. Hun Sen seized full control in a 1997 coup, the 2013 election was marred by fraud claims and deadly crackdowns, the main opposition, the Cambodia National Rescue Party, was dissolved in 2017, and its successor, the Candlelight Party, was disqualified in 2023. The CPP draws legitimacy from Angkor-era nationalism and Buddhism, and holds power through patronage linking the army, courts and business, which also let elites control privatization of land and state firms.

Fourth, China. Beijing once backed the Khmer Rouge, but after the 1997 coup, when the West cut aid and ASEAN delayed Cambodia's entry, China stepped in. It now provides most foreign investment, built roads, a special economic zone in Sihanoukville and dams, holds a large share of Cambodia's public debt, and has access to the Ream naval base on the Gulf of Thailand, with joint military drills. In return, Cambodia defends Chinese positions at the UN and inside ASEAN: in 2016 it blocked an ASEAN statement on China's island-building and received large Chinese loans soon after. For China, Cambodia is a counterweight to Vietnam and the Philippines, a possible base near the South China Sea, and a reliable vote in ASEAN. Still, Cambodia's constitution proclaims neutrality, the U.S. and EU are its main export markets, and it co-sponsored the UN resolution condemning Russia's invasion of Ukraine.

Fifth, the economy. Cambodia relies on cheap labor in garment factories, which employ about six hundred thousand people and generate most export earnings. It is slowly reducing its dependence on the U.S. dollar by promoting the riel, and hopes to move into electronics and solar panels as companies diversify away from China.

Since the file was written: Cambodia has drawn global attention for huge online scam compounds run by criminal networks, leading to U.S. and British sanctions, and in 2025 deadly border clashes with Thailand broke out over disputed temples, with repeated ceasefires.
