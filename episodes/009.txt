Episode 9. Sanctions.
Today: how to model sanctions, the case against them, and the case for them.

First, modeling. Imagine a multilateral coalition with secondary sanctions on anyone who helps the target, and clear conditions for lifting them step by step. The main types are targeted sanctions on elites, like asset freezes and travel bans; economic sanctions, like embargoes; and financial sanctions, like cutting banks off SWIFT or from the U.S. dollar.

Now, against sanctions.

They are too weak. Targets build resilience by finding new partners, rogue states sanction-proof their economies in advance, coalition members cheat, and domestic politics waters sanctions down until fatigue sets in.

Targets will not give in. Complying once invites more sanctions, anti-Western leaders cannot look weak, and they expect to outlast the coalition.

They worsen repression. Ordinary people suffer more than elites, leaders blame the West for every hardship and paint protesters as foreign agents, states turn to worse partners, and the middle class that might push for reform never develops. State firms and loyal oligarchs actually gain market share.

They hurt innocents. Humanitarian aid slows down under red tape and fear of legal penalties.

The record is grim. Six decades of embargo on Cuba have not changed the regime. North Korea built ICBMs despite UN sanctions full of Chinese and Russian loopholes. Sanctions on Haiti in the nineties caused power outages that crippled hospitals. In Iraq after 1990, child mortality doubled, and a UN official resigned saying the program met the definition of genocide. In Angola, UNITA simply sold more blood diamonds.

Now, for sanctions.

They work by creating leverage for negotiations. Targeted sanctions hit self-interested elites where it hurts, and once one elite defects, others follow. Economic pain undermines a regime's legitimacy, businesses push for a deal, and protest movements gain energy.

They matter morally. Impunity emboldens violators, sanctions cut off weapons and financing, and they signal that a regime is illegitimate.

And they beat the alternatives. Military action kills more people, leaves power vacuums that breed terrorism, and rarely has lasting political support. Diplomacy without sanctions is all carrot and no stick.

Successes exist. Aid cuts pushed Malawi to adopt multi-party elections in the nineties. Divestment and sanctions sent capital fleeing South Africa and helped force negotiations with the ANC. EU pressure made Rwanda stop backing the M23 rebels. And in Guatemala in 1993, the threat of an embargo forced a president who tried a self-coup to resign within two weeks.
