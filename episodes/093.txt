Episode 93. Labor Markets.
Today: why labor markets fail, why it matters, and three remedies: unions, labor laws and collective ownership, plus the 2023 auto workers' strike.

First, five market failures. One, power imbalances: a company barely notices losing one cashier, but the cashier loses everything, so workers stay silent; some farms exploit undocumented workers who fear deportation, and persistent unemployment means there is always someone desperate for an entry-level job. Two, information gaps: pay secrecy and urgent needs, like next month's rent, push people into bad contracts. Three, barriers to entry, like geography or young workers lacking experience. Four, wages ignore social value: a banker earns many times a teacher, nurse or garbage collector, despite their huge benefits to society. Five, bias: diversity helps firms, but individual managers carry prejudices, and executive pay is shaped by CEOs' influence over the committees that set it.

Second, why it matters. Exploitation means poverty, which brings food and housing insecurity, worse health and damaged families, and becomes self-perpetuating through debt, untreated illness and poor neighborhoods. Society pays through crime, overloaded public services and taxpayers effectively subsidizing low wages. Sociologist Matthew Desmond argues a living wage works like medicine for stress and health. It also wastes talent, bloats executive pay, lowers morale and raises turnover, and rigid hierarchies bury good ideas from the shop floor.

Third, unions. Collective bargaining gives workers strength in numbers, and unions won the forty-hour week, lobby for laws, help enforce safety rules, and raise pay even for non-members. But U.S. membership fell from about a third of workers in the fifties to around one in ten, and far fewer in the private sector. Reagan's breaking of the 1981 air traffic controllers' strike was a turning point, followed by union-busting, reclassifying workers as contractors and anti-union laws, as seen with Amazon and Starbucks. Decline feeds on itself, since workers will not pay dues to weak unions. Unions also suffer corruption, past exclusion of women and Black workers, competition between unions, automation, outsourcing and government opposition.

In 2023 the United Auto Workers under Shawn Fain won a six-week strike against GM, Ford and Stellantis, securing raises of about twenty-five percent over four years and cost-of-living adjustments, with Biden joining a picket line, and vowed to organize non-union plants like Tesla and Toyota. Critics feared higher car prices and a slower EV transition.

Fourth, labor laws. Governments can mandate rather than negotiate, protect all workers, level the field so no firm loses an edge, and are better funded. But lobbying and regulatory capture weaken them, agencies are underfunded, lawyers find loopholes faster than regulators close them, compliance can be faked when workers fear reporting, compliance costs hit small businesses, and laws can be repealed.

Fifth, collective ownership. Full Marxism means seizing the means of production, but central planning has caused shortages and authoritarianism, and land seizures in Mugabe's Zimbabwe contributed to famine. Milder forms include worker seats on boards, as in Germany, nationalization, and worker cooperatives. These give workers a voice, but board representatives can be powerless, state firms become corrupt and inefficient, and co-ops sometimes cut wages in crises. After COVID, tight labor markets and renewed interest in unions shifted some power back to workers.
