Episode 72. Politics of Central Banking.
Today: rules versus discretion, central bank independence, mistakes central banks make, and Argentina's economy.

First, the pre-2008 consensus was simple: independent technocrats, focused on inflation targets. The financial crisis shattered it.

Second, rules or discretion? Rules win on predictability: discretion is inconsistent over time, forward guidance gets reversed, households and investors need certainty, rules are easier to explain, protect banks from political meddling, make deviations obvious, and stop bankers from becoming unelected multipurpose rulers. Discretion wins on flexibility: rules are rigid, built on past experience and blind to new shocks like pandemics, while reputational concerns keep discretion disciplined.

Third, independence. For it: independent central banks have better records, like Germany's Bundesbank. Parties gain from stable policy and want to tie the hands of future governments. The seventies inflation was a painful lesson, and independence attracts foreign investment. Politicians are short-termist, inexpert and tempted to promise cheap money and renege later. And independent banks remain accountable, since legislatures can rewrite their mandates; as Paul Volcker noted, Congress created the Fed and can uncreate it. Against it: narrow mandates, like the ECB's focus only on inflation, hamper crisis response. Monetary policy involves real trade-offs, like inflation versus jobs, which deserve democratic input, especially since quantitative easing hugely expanded central bank power and picks winners through what it buys. Some scholars argue independence is a myth anyway, given political appointments and ties to finance. And central banks matter more than ever because fiscal stimulus often disappoints: people save temporary windfalls and anticipate future taxes.

Fourth, mistakes. Central bankers suffer from biases, groupthink and flawed models. Japan's slow response worsened its lost decade. Before 2008, Greenspan kept rates far too low for too long, fueling the housing bubble. And when the crisis hit, the Fed treated it as a liquidity problem when the real issue was banks not trusting each other.

Finally, Argentina. It has defaulted nine times. Under Macri it took a record fifty-seven billion dollar IMF loan in 2018, his successor printed money, and in 2022 it signed another forty-four billion dollar deal. By 2023 inflation was well above a hundred percent and about forty percent of people were poor. Farming is a small share of GDP but over half of exports. Peronism, built on social justice, sovereignty and economic independence, dominates politics. The history: an early export boom, then Perón's protectionism, repeated military coups, the eighties hyperinflation, a dollar peg in 1991 that collapsed in 2001 with frozen bank accounts. In 2023, Javier Milei, a libertarian outsider, won the presidency promising to abolish the central bank, dollarize the economy, slash spending and halve the number of ministries.
