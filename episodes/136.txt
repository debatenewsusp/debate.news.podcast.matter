Episode 136. Brunei.
Today: Brunei's absolute monarchy, oil economy and balancing act between China and the U.S.

First, politics. Brunei became independent in 1984 and has been ruled by a sultanate for centuries. Sultan Hassanal Bolkiah has reigned since 1967, promoting a Malay Muslim Monarchy. In 2014 Brunei adopted Sharia criminal law, the first in East Asia, with punishments like stoning for adultery and amputation for theft. In 2019 it made gay sex punishable by death, but after global condemnation, the Sultan extended a moratorium on executions. Malays, about two-thirds of the population, enjoy privileges in land, jobs and education, while most ethnic Chinese, about a tenth, are only permanent residents, denied citizenship benefits.

Second, why the Sultan is popular. There is no income tax; healthcare, education and housing are free; income per person is among the world's highest; corruption is low; women do well in education and work. But the media is heavily censored, and the royal family holds enormous private wealth.

Third, the economy. Oil and gas make up about sixty percent of GDP and over ninety percent of exports, making Brunei Southeast Asia's second richest country after Singapore, with its currency pegged to Singapore's dollar. Reserves may run dry within three decades, so Vision 2035 aims to diversify, including into petrochemicals, led by the mostly Chinese-owned Hengyi Industries. About a quarter of workers are migrants from neighboring countries.

Fourth, foreign policy. Brunei is ASEAN's smallest member by population but active. It is close to Malaysia and Singapore, whose army trains Brunei's forces in exchange for a jungle training camp. It hedges: with the U.S., it has a defense agreement, joint exercises, supported the Trans-Pacific Partnership and condemned Russia's invasion of Ukraine; with China, trade has quadrupled in a decade and Chinese firms invest billions. But in the South China Sea, Brunei rejects China's nine-dash line, upholds the UN Convention on the Law of the Sea, and quietly disputes Chinese claims to Louisa Reef and Rifleman Bank inside its exclusive economic zone, where its offshore energy lies.
