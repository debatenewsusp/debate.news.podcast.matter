Episode 135. South Korea.
Today: how the two Koreas diverged, South Korea's economic miracle and democratization, North Korea's survival, and the politics of the peninsula.

First, division. Japan ruled Korea brutally from 1910 to 1945, banning the Korean language and exploiting its economy. After Japan's defeat, the U.S. and the Soviet Union split the peninsula at the thirty-eighth parallel. In the North, the Soviets installed Kim Il Sung, who built a command economy, one-party rule and a personality cult. In the South, a clumsy U.S. military government suppressed uprisings before elections brought Syngman Rhee to power. Two states were founded in 1948. In 1950, with Stalin and Mao's backing, Kim invaded the South; the UN, led by the U.S., defended it. The 1953 armistice left the border near where it began, with a demilitarized zone but no peace treaty.

Second, the North. Kim promoted juche, self-reliance, rejecting dependence on great powers, but isolation left inefficient industry. The state militarized everything out of paranoia about invasion, saturated life with propaganda, and after a debt crisis in the seventies sold missiles to countries like Iran, Syria and Pakistan. The Soviet collapse in 1991 cut off aid and cheap energy, Kim Il Sung died in 1994, and a devastating famine followed. His son Kim Jong Il adopted songun, military first. In 1994 Pyongyang agreed with the U.S. to freeze its nuclear program in exchange for help building civilian reactors, and South Korea's Sunshine Policy brought aid, tourism to Mount Kumgang and the joint Kaesong industrial zone. But investors stayed away, Bush named North Korea in the Axis of Evil in 2002, and Pyongyang left the Non-Proliferation Treaty in 2003 and tested a nuclear bomb in 2006. Kim Jong Un, in power since 2011, allowed small market reforms but expanded the nuclear program.

Third, China's support. Beijing keeps the regime alive through trade because it fears a refugee crisis, fears nuclear chaos near its border, opposes a unified Korea allied with the U.S., and benefits from cheap North Korean labor.

Fourth, the South's miracle. After Rhee's autocratic, aid-dependent rule ended in protests in 1960, General Park Chung-hee took power in a coup. Copying Japan, the state set production targets with family-run conglomerates called chaebol, like Samsung, Hyundai and LG. State-owned banks steered them, for example threatening Hyundai into shipbuilding. Imports were restricted, Japanese reparations funded steel, and land reform, housing, schooling and rural support spread the gains, partly to blunt communism. Incomes grew about seven percent a year for three decades: the Miracle on the Han River. The other Asian Tigers, Taiwan, Hong Kong and Singapore, used similar tools: national champions, export orientation, state investment in infrastructure and skills, incentives for foreign investment, and land reform.

Fifth, democracy. Park ruled by decree from 1972 and was assassinated in 1979; another military regime followed, crushing protests. Mass protests in June 1987 forced free elections, and former dictators were later prosecuted. The 1997 Asian financial crisis forced reforms of the chaebol, but ties between business and politics remain close. Former Hyundai executive Lee Myung-bak became president and was later convicted of corruption, and Park Geun-hye was impeached in 2017 after huge candlelight protests over bribery. Meanwhile, K-pop and K-dramas became soft power, even smuggled into the North, which punishes them harshly.

Sixth, today. The two big parties are the center-left Democratic Party and the conservative People Power Party. Society remains socially conservative and anti-communist, and support for engagement with the North has faded amid missile tests. South Korea is Asia's fourth-largest economy, built on chips, cars and petrochemicals, and vulnerable to global trade swings. Despite historic anger at Japan, Seoul joined Tokyo and Washington at the Camp David summit in August 2023, agreeing to consult on shared threats and share missile warning data.

Since the file was written: President Yoon Suk Yeol declared martial law in December 2024, was impeached and removed from office, and Democratic Party leader Lee Jae-myung won the June 2025 election. North Korea has declared the South a hostile state and abandoned the goal of reunification.

That was South Korea. Next episode: Brunei.
