Episode 100. Israeli Judicial Reforms.
Today: the 2023 fight over Israel's judiciary: the context, the reasonableness doctrine, both sides, and the reaction.

First, context. The November 2022 election gave Netanyahu's Likud and its religious and far-right allies, including United Torah Judaism, Shas, Religious Zionism and Otzma Yehudit, sixty-four seats in the hundred-and-twenty-seat Knesset, likely the most right-wing and religious government in Israel's history. The right had been growing because of much higher birth rates among ultra-Orthodox Jews, security fears after the 2021 Gaza conflict, little contact between Israelis and Palestinians, young voters leaning right, and fears about Israel's Jewish identity after an Arab party joined the previous coalition.

Second, the reform. Israel has no written constitution, only Basic Laws, so courts rarely strike down laws as unconstitutional. Instead they use a doctrine of reasonableness: did a decision weigh all the relevant factors properly? Courts used it to block ministerial appointments and decisions. The government's plan, led by Justice Minister Yariv Levin, first sought political control over appointing judges, an end to review of Knesset laws, and the power to override the Supreme Court by simple majority. Mass protests forced a pause in March 2023. Then in July, the Knesset passed the law abolishing the reasonableness standard, sixty-four to zero, as the opposition boycotted the vote.

Third, supporters' arguments: unelected judges hold too much power, the reasonableness standard is vague and not written in law, and courts have favored secular and left-wing interests, for example on West Bank settlements and ultra-Orthodox exemptions from military service.

Fourth, opponents' arguments: removing judicial checks threatens democracy, Netanyahu could use a weakened judiciary to escape his own bribery and fraud trial, for example by replacing the attorney general, and the far-right coalition could roll back civil liberties and impose a religious vision of the state. The newspaper Haaretz called it a judicial coup.

Finally, the reaction. Hundreds of thousands protested, elite pilots and tens of thousands of reservists threatened to stop serving, hospitals and businesses joined strikes, opposition leader Benny Gantz vowed to reverse the law, and the White House called it unfortunate. Since the file was written, Israel's Supreme Court struck the law down in January 2024, and the October 7 attacks and the Gaza war transformed Israeli politics.
