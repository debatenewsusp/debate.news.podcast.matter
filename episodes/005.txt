Episode 5. Too Big To Fail.
Five parts today: what systemically important banks are, breaking them up, nationalizing them, defending them, and bailouts.

First, the definitions. Global systemically important banks, or G-SIBs, include names like JPMorgan, HSBC, Citi, Goldman and BNP Paribas. Regulators judge them on size, cross-border activity, interconnectedness, how hard they are to replace, and complexity. The problem is moral hazard: if markets expect a bank to be rescued, it takes more risk and taxpayers carry the cost.

Second, breaking them up.

The model is to split them into investment partnerships, wholesale banks and retail banks. Why? Moral hazard pushes them toward excessive risk. They are so interconnected that trouble at one triggers panic and fire sales everywhere. Implicit state backing gives them cheap credit, so smaller banks cannot compete. And small banks are actually good: they know local clients, cannot gamble with exotic products, and can still pool risk through loan syndication. Regulation alone fails, because lobbying guts rules, lawyers find loopholes, and regulators get poached.

Third, nationalizing them.

Government will argue that profit is the root problem. A state-run bank answers to voters, so it is less likely to exploit consumers with predatory lending or inflated fees. It must be transparent, which helps regulators and central banks. Parties need a long-term reputation, so capital flows to long-term projects like infrastructure. Crises become rarer, because state banks are more risk-averse, and easier to manage, because the government can coordinate one unified response. Expect Opposition to say corruption, bureaucracy and lack of talent; the answer is intense media scrutiny, internal watchdogs, and the fact that the state already runs effective agencies like central banks.

Fourth, the case that big banks are good.

They are safer: they pool deposits, hold bigger capital buffers, borrow cheaply in a crisis and diversify across markets. They face the toughest oversight, like stress tests, Dodd-Frank and Basel Three. Moral hazard is overstated, because reputation, competition from hedge funds and other lenders, and internal career incentives all punish reckless risk. Small banks are actually more fragile: they rely on debt rather than equity and suffer maturity mismatches when short-term rates move. Big banks are also efficient and innovative, which lowers fees and widens access. And breaking them up sends a scary signal that could trigger bank runs.

Finally, bailouts.

For: they prevent contagion, stop systemic collapse and signal that the state will act. Governments can attach conditions, like replacing the executives. Against: they reward recklessness, transfer money from taxpayers to the wealthy, and fuel populist anger. Key examples are the New Deal and the Home Owners' Loan Corporation in the Depression, the Reconstruction Finance Corporation, which became a warning about political favoritism, and the 700 billion dollar TARP program after 2008.
