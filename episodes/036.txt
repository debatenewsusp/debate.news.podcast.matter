Episode 36. War Reporting.
Today: why war journalism matters, why it is so hard, and the ethics of war itself, known as Just War Theory.

First, why it matters. Accurate reporting lets the public judge military operations, raises awareness of crises like Syria, Yemen and Myanmar, and drives support for aid and peacekeeping. Citizens have a right to know how their military uses force. Documented evidence of atrocities, like Srebrenica, makes later prosecutions and truth commissions possible. And it counters wartime propaganda.

Second, why it is hard. Militaries depend on secrecy and journalism on publicity; during the Falklands War, British officials said exactly that. The work is deadly: crossfire, shelling and minefields kill reporters, communications get jammed, and despite protection under international law, journalists are targeted. The Taliban offered bounties on Western reporters, ISIS filmed beheadings, and American journalist Brent Renaud was killed in Ukraine two weeks into the invasion. And truth is the first casualty of war: governments push propaganda and hide abuses, and militaries control access, blacklist outlets and pressure reporters. Famous war journalists include Marguerite Higgins in Korea, Walter Cronkite in Vietnam and Anthony Shadid in Iraq and Libya.

Third, Just War Theory. Wars are often unavoidable, so the question is how to limit their barbarity. There are three parts: the right to go to war, right conduct in war, and justice after war.

The right to go to war requires a just cause, meaning self-defense or humanitarian intervention against atrocities, with no hidden motives; last resort, after diplomacy fails; a public declaration by a legitimate authority; proportionality between the cause and the means; and a reasonable chance of success.

Right conduct requires discrimination between combatants and civilians, since soldiers accept the risks of the ring and civilians do not, with responsibility growing the closer someone is to the war machine; proportionality, so force never exceeds the military advantage; and necessity, using the least harmful means.

Preemptive war is contested. For: striking first may prevent a bloodier war and deters aggression. Against: states are innocent until they attack, arming is not attacking, and pretexts are easy to fabricate.

Critics say there is no just war, because war is hell; that morality does not apply in war; or, as consequentialists argue, that whatever ends war fastest is justified.

Historically, Thomas Aquinas laid out three conditions: sovereign authority, just cause and right intention. The Geneva Conventions, starting in 1864 and expanded in 1949, codified protections. In his 2009 Nobel speech, Obama captured the tension: force is sometimes necessary and morally justified, since nonviolence could not have stopped Hitler, yet war is always a human tragedy and never glorious.
