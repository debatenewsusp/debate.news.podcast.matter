Episode 76. Journalism.
Today: media as gatekeepers, why reporting is biased, and who becomes a journalist.

First, gatekeeping. News organizations decide which stories deserve resources, reporters' backgrounds filter how events are covered, and stories pass up a hierarchy where senior editors decide what runs, where it goes and what the headline says. Journalists are selective intermediaries between events and audiences.

Second, bias. It can mean distorting facts, favoring one side, or quietly choosing which stories to cover at all. Why does it happen?

Demand: readers prefer news that confirms their views and takes little effort, outlets target niche audiences, and brands build reputations, like Fox News on the right. Supply: owners have their own political interests.

Consolidation: the 1947 Hutchins Commission warned that concentrated media ownership homogenizes coverage and stifles dissent.

Socialization: bias is rarely ordered from above; it is absorbed. Staff study their own paper to learn what gets the front page and how groups are described. Editors' corrections signal what is welcome. Unwritten agreements rule some topics out. And senior editors control promotions, assignments and layoffs and are admired by young reporters, so their leanings trickle down.

Quantity over quality: outlets must fill constant airtime and compete, but real news is scarce, so dull stories get hyped.

Government: dependence on subsidies breeds deference, censorship and intimidation persist even in democracies, and nationalism colors foreign coverage, as with Israel and Palestine.

Third, journalists themselves. Most are university graduates who entered a competitive field because they love the craft, not the modest pay. The appeal is excitement, interviewing the powerful, witnessing history, variety, and the pride of being part of the Fourth Estate whose work shapes public debate.
