Episode 14. Urbanization.
Two sides today: urbanization is good, and urbanization is bad.

First, urbanization is good.

Cities drive development. Deep labor pools attract foreign investment. Clustering firms together creates agglomeration economies: lower transport costs, easier access to inputs, knowledge spillovers and specialization. A recognizable central business district helps poor countries overcome investment stigma, and the big consumer market, business-friendly city governments and supporting firms like insurers and law firms make investing easier. Foreign investment then brings jobs, training, exports and technology.

Cities deliver services efficiently. Hospitals and schools reach far more people when costs are spread across dense populations, which matters most for cash-strapped governments. Better education builds a skilled workforce over time.

Rural areas benefit too, through remittances and skilled people moving back and forth. When private services become profitable in cities, governments can redirect their budgets to the countryside. Cities can also help the environment through efficient land use and less driving, and they tend to be more progressive, more entrepreneurial and better for workers' skills.

Now, urbanization is bad.

It drains rural areas. Educated youth, middle-class wealth and voters leave. Businesses close, tax revenues fall, farms lose labor and national policy tilts toward cities.

Remittances are a weak fix. Fees eat a chunk of them, communities become dependent, people work and save less, and money gets spent on goods made elsewhere.

It can hurt development. Rapid growth overloads ports, roads, power, water and sanitation, and visible infrastructure failures scare investors. A huge supply of cheap labor reduces the incentive to innovate.

It raises crime. Migration outpaces job creation, slums fill with unemployed youth, inequality is highly visible, anonymity weakens social accountability, and uprooted migrants seek community in gangs. Crime then deters investment by raising security and insurance costs.

It harms quality of life. Migrants end up in slums with poor sanitation, disease spreads in crowded conditions, services cannot keep up, housing is scarce and expensive, and many end up in the informal economy. Internal migration also often becomes a first step toward emigration abroad, worsening brain drain.

Finally, rural resentment of urban elites fuels political backlash, and urban sprawl adds pollution and deforestation.
