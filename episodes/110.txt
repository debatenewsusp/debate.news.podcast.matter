Episode 110. China's Slowdown.
Today: why China's economy slowed in 2023, why the usual explanations fall short, what is really happening, and the fall of Evergrande.

First, three conventional explanations, and the rebuttals.

One, a debt trap, like Japan's 1990s balance sheet recession. But Japan's bubble was bigger, and its banks and companies owned each other's shares, so everything fell together. China's debt is more fragmented across regions and sectors, private firms have been deleveraging for a decade, and Xi has made financial risk a priority.

Two, lost confidence: entrepreneurs and consumers no longer trust a system where Leninist politics overrides economic dynamism. But low consumption is mainly about income: during the lockdowns the government gave very little stimulus or welfare. And investment is still strong in areas like electric cars, where China now leads world car exports.

Three, mismanagement: weak COVID stimulus, a chaotic exit from zero-COVID and falling exports, so the fix is simply more stimulus.

Second, what is really happening. The party's priorities changed. Stability used to come first and growth second as a tool for stability. Xi instead prioritizes security, technological self-sufficiency and social equity, even at the cost of slower growth, and holds an almost puritanical view that austerity is good for people. He is deliberately shifting capital out of exhausted property and infrastructure sectors into high-tech industry. The implications: China is not collapsing but suffers chronic weak demand, because the party sees the economy as about production, not consumption, which is why it runs constant trade surpluses. The era of directional liberalism is over: in the eighties and nineties entrepreneurs invested because leaders signaled more openness ahead, while today the direction points toward more control and one-man rule, sapping dynamism. But manufacturing stays strong, because factories and their know-how cannot vanish overnight and are more insulated from Xi's crackdowns than tech platforms.

Finally, Evergrande. Riding the housing boom that began with 1990s reforms, it grew to around thirteen hundred projects in hundreds of cities and expanded into electric cars, theme parks and football. Its model was borrowing heavily and pre-selling homes before building them. When Beijing's three red lines capped developers' debt, the model broke, and in 2021 it defaulted. Its value was almost entirely wiped out, it sought bankruptcy protection in the U.S., and in 2024 a Hong Kong court ordered it liquidated.
