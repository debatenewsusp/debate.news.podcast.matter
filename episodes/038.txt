Episode 38. Carceral Feminism.
Today: what carceral feminism is, and the case against it.

First, what it is: tackling violence against women mainly through police, prosecution and prison. The U.S. Violence Against Women Act of 1994 put over a billion dollars into investigating and prosecuting these crimes. Similar laws exist in the U.K., India and Spain, and some cities classify misogyny as a hate crime.

Now, the case against.

It ignores police violence, especially against poor, minority and queer women. Mandatory arrest laws often lead to both partners being arrested when police cannot tell who the aggressor is. In the U.S., the number of incarcerated women grew enormously in the years after the act.

Prison does not help women. Mass incarceration has done little to reduce sexual violence, and prisons breed abuse, addiction and radicalization, so men may come out more dangerous.

It diverts money from what works: shelters, public housing and welfare that let women escape abusive relationships, and it ignores the economic inequality that drives gender-based violence.

It is co-opted by conservative, tough-on-crime politics, which uses feminism's credibility to justify harsher sentences, more police power and bigger prison budgets, while the state quietly cuts support for women's shelters.

It leaves vulnerable women behind. Undocumented women who report abuse risk deportation, poor women risk eviction for calling the police, and women who fight back against abusers face harsh sentences. Fewer than one in seven victims report to police globally, and those who do often endure retraumatizing trials. Abusers can even manipulate the system to get their victims arrested.

Finally, it is bad for feminism itself. It frames violence as an individual problem solved by calling the police, rather than a community one. It professionalizes the movement and alienates grassroots organizers. And it puts feminism at odds with abolitionist movements and pushes away women of color, who see it backing institutions that have long oppressed their communities.
