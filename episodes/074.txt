Episode 74. Sweatshops and Child Labor.
Two topics today: sweatshops and child labor, and then antinatalism, the view that having children is wrong.

First, labor regulation comes in four forms: individual rights like minimum wages and safety rules, collective rights like unions, job security, and anti-discrimination law.

Second, two case studies on child labor bans backfiring. In Bangladesh in the early nineties, a proposed U.S. ban on imports made with child labor led garment factories to fire around fifty thousand children. UNICEF and Oxfam later found most had not gone to school: they ended up begging, scavenging, in unregistered workshops and stone-crushing, and many girls were pushed into prostitution. In Sialkot, Pakistan, which makes most of the world's footballs, a 1990s campaign ended child stitching, but children moved into brutal brick kilns, few stayed in school, and women's employment in the industry collapsed.

Third, why child labor laws fail. Enforcement targets employers, but the root is supply: poor families need the income, so banning it in one place shifts it elsewhere. Labor agencies are underfunded, like Ghana's, despite a 1998 ban. Cultural norms sometimes condone it. Most child labor is on remote farms that are hard to monitor. Subcontracting hides it from Western brands, as with Nike's footballs. And enforcement pushes children into the informal economy, where wages are lower and abuse is worse.

Fourth, the case for sweatshops. They pay more than workers' alternatives, usually subsistence farming, and long lines of applicants show people prefer them. Income is steadier, they employ many women, and some offer benefits. Paul Krugman, while no fan of their owners, argued low-wage factory work drives industrialization, as in Indonesia, where child malnutrition fell sharply as manufacturing grew. Low labor costs attract multinationals, which bring skills, technology, foreign currency, follow-on investment, infrastructure and cheaper goods.

Now, antinatalism. It was popularized by Schopenhauer: suffering is built into life, birth is nonconsensual, and only the instinct to survive makes us reproduce. Studies find antinatalists are not especially depressed. Strands include ecological, philanthropic, misanthropic, selective and consent-based views.

The arguments. Philanthropic: life is mostly suffering. Our capacity for pain exceeds our capacity for pleasure, and evolution makes us focus on pain. There is an asymmetry, from philosopher David Benatar: the absence of pain is good even if no one exists to enjoy it, but the absence of pleasure is only bad if someone is deprived of it, so not creating life avoids pain at no cost. Happiness is relative and scarce, and the world is full of poverty, war and climate disaster. Why do people say their lives are worth living? Because we are wired to value survival and cannot imagine not existing.

Misanthropic: humans cause enormous harm, through genocide, war and everyday cruelty, and in a zero-sum world every win is someone else's loss, so creating new humans creates more harm.

Consent: children cannot agree to be born, and parents have no right to gamble with someone else's suffering.

Environmental: humans destroy habitats, drive extinctions and factory-farm billions of animals, so procreation harms the natural world.

Finally, exploitation arguments: infancy is mostly distress, and pregnancy imposes huge physical costs on women.
