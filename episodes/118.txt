Episode 118. Ukraine.
Today: a snapshot of the war at the end of 2023: why the counteroffensive failed, Western weapons, and the strategic picture.

First, the 2023 counteroffensive. After Russia, led by the Wagner Group, took Bakhmut in May, Ukraine attacked in June along three axes in Donetsk and Zaporizhzhia, aiming to cut the land bridge to Crimea and reach the Sea of Azov. It gained only small pieces of territory at a high cost, nothing like the sweeping Kharkiv and Kherson victories of 2022. Why?

One, Russian defenses: deep minefields, trenches, dragon's teeth and bunkers known as the Surovikin Line, while Western mine-clearing vehicles were large, loud and easily destroyed, and many donated tanks were lost in the first weeks. Two, delays: shortages of ammunition and trained brigades pushed the start from spring to June, giving Russia time to fortify, as Zelensky admitted. Three, tactics: Ukraine had trained for defense, brief Western training did not stick, timing and choice of axes were questionable, command remained rigid and Soviet-style, and Russia reportedly cracked some communications. Meanwhile, Russia adapted, with better camouflage, fortifications, communications and logistics, plus effective local counterattacks under General Gerasimov's active defense. Four, attacks were too small, platoons with a couple of tanks rather than massed assaults.

Second, Western weapons: Leopard, Challenger and Abrams tanks and Bradley vehicles; air defenses like Patriot, NASAMS, IRIS-T and Gepard against Russian missiles and drones; HIMARS and other precision artillery; anti-tank and anti-air missiles like Javelin and Stinger; controversial cluster munitions from the U.S.; and, eventually, F-16 jets. The F-16 debate: skeptics said training would take too long, Russian air defenses would deny air superiority, maintenance is costly and Ukraine's rough runways are unsuitable. Supporters said Ukraine's Soviet-era fleet was shrinking and short of spare parts, and F-16s would add firepower, morale and long-term deterrence.

Third, the end of 2023. Sixty billion dollars in U.S. aid was stuck in Congress over Republican demands on immigration, Hungary blocked an EU package even as the EU opened membership talks, and Ukraine faced exhausted troops, a lack of experienced sergeants, a costly assault across the Dnipro and dwindling ammunition. Putin believed he could outlast the West, ordered a large troop increase and reorganized military districts facing NATO. A Russian victory would put battle-hardened forces on NATO's borders.

Fourth, Putin's grip. After Prigozhin's mutiny in June 2023, Putin purged allies, absorbed Wagner into the army, and Prigozhin died in a suspicious plane crash in August. His rule rests on patronage networks and elites who need him in power. Sanctions hit oligarchs, but Russia evaded export controls through China and former Soviet states, stabilized the ruble and redirected oil to China and India, though the sixty-dollar price cap squeezed revenues.

Since the file was written, Congress approved the aid package in April 2024, and fighting and peace efforts have continued, so check the latest developments.

That was Ukraine. Next episode: Russia.
