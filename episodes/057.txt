Episode 57. Space Exploration.
Today: how rockets work, why space matters, who the players are, and the case against space tourism.

First, rocket science. Rockets rely on Newton's laws: burning propellant, a mix of fuel and oxidizer, produces hot exhaust that shoots out of a nozzle, and the reaction pushes the rocket up. Thrust depends on exhaust speed and how much mass is expelled, and it must exceed the rocket's weight to lift off and then reach escape velocity, around forty thousand kilometers per hour. Swiveling nozzles steer it. Everything must be perfect: a leak, clog, tiny manufacturing error or vibration problem can doom a mission.

Second, why it matters. It drives scientific discovery about planets, black holes and possible life. It produces technology like satellite communications, GPS, weather forecasting and medical imaging. It inspires future scientists; Apollo 11 is credited with a surge in science and engineering PhDs. It builds cooperation even between rivals, like Russians and Americans on the International Space Station. It protects humanity's long-term survival through asteroid defense, environmental monitoring and maybe settling Mars. And it makes us reflect on our place in the cosmos.

Third, the players. NASA works closely with contractors like Boeing and Lockheed Martin. Private firms include SpaceX, with its Falcon rockets, Starship and Starlink; Blue Origin, Jeff Bezos's company; and Virgin Galactic, Richard Branson's tourism venture. Public agencies include NASA, the European Space Agency, Russia's Roscosmos and China's space agency, all with limited budgets. Space tourism today is only suborbital.

Fourth, the case against space tourism. It is the ultimate inequality, with tickets costing hundreds of thousands of dollars. It adds to space junk, already tens of thousands of objects in orbit, risking a cascade of collisions known as Kessler syndrome. It harms the environment through emissions and damage to the ozone layer. And safety is a concern, with little regulation, profit pressure and billionaire rivalries.
