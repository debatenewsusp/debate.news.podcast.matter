Episode 6. Free Trade.
Two sides today: the case for free trade, and the case for protectionism.

First, free trade.

State protection distorts markets. Firms lobby for favors instead of investing, and inefficient companies survive on government support. Infant industries that get protection grow dependent and never grow up. And governments are bad at picking winners: well-connected firms capture the benefits, elections reward popular but unproductive sectors, and nobody can predict the global economy anyway.

Free trade drives specialization, lowers prices, raises disposable income and widens the economic pie. It also reduces conflict. Countries do not bomb their own supply chains or their own customers, sanctions bite harder on integrated economies, and trade gives peaceful access to resources.

Protection even hurts the infant industries it claims to help. Shielding steel makes steel more expensive for every manufacturer that uses it. Retaliatory tariffs close export markets. Protected firms often just copy foreign products and sell them at higher prices, as Brazilian computer makers did with Apple designs in the eighties. Exposure to global competition, technology and capital is what actually makes firms better.

And there is a liberty argument: governments should not decide how people spend the fruits of their labor or who they can trade with.

Now, protectionism.

First, further liberalization is mostly unnecessary. Decades of trade rounds and free trade agreements have pushed average tariffs to around one or two percent almost everywhere. What remains are non-tariff barriers, like health, safety, environmental and cultural rules. Those are hard to negotiate, deeply tied to legitimate domestic policy, and removing them attacks democratic choices like worker protections or rules on GMOs.

Second, development. Infant industries do need time and subsidies, regional blocs need external barriers, and poor governments without a tax base rely on tariff revenue.

Third, markets ignore social and security concerns. National security is a real reason to protect key sectors, which is the logic behind America's CHIPS Act.

Fourth, trade only benefits everyone if winners compensate losers, and that rarely happens. Governments cannot easily identify who lost out, since deindustrialization is gradual. The marginal gains from more liberalization are small while inequality rises. Winning industries use their wealth to lobby against welfare. And by the time workers are hurt, they have lost their political power.

Finally, ripping out protections often causes political upheaval, fuels right-wing populism, scares away investors and destabilizes institutions.
