Episode 4. Interest Rates.
Four parts today: the Fed's position, the case for lowering rates, the case for raising them, and high versus low rates in general.

First, context. This file was written in late 2023. At that point the Fed had raised rates eleven times since March 2022, from zero to around five and a quarter percent. Headline inflation had fallen from a peak near nine percent to under four.

Now, the case for lowering rates.

Inflation is no longer the main threat. Supply chains have recovered, energy prices cooled, pandemic stimulus has worked through the system, and past hikes are still taking effect.

Counterintuitively, lower rates can even reduce inflation. Fixing supply chains requires investment, which high borrowing costs block. High rates also crush small businesses, which leads to consolidation and less competition. And because energy is priced in dollars, a stronger dollar from rate hikes makes imports pricier elsewhere.

High rates hurt ordinary people. Mortgages, student loans and car loans all get more expensive, and firms that cannot borrow short-term start laying off workers.

They also threaten financial stability. Silicon Valley Bank showed how rising rates erode the value of bonds on bank balance sheets. More hikes signal trouble, banks tighten credit, and panic can spread.

And they hurt developing countries, whose debts are often in dollars. Higher U.S. rates mean a stronger dollar and capital flight from emerging markets.

Now, the case for raising rates.

Inflation is not tamed yet: labor markets are hot and consumer demand is strong. Cutting too early reignites price pressure. Central banks also need credibility; if they abandon their mandate, their forward guidance stops working. And cheap money fuels asset bubbles and pushes investors into riskier bets.

Finally, high versus low rates in general.

High rates cool an overheated economy, limit excessive leverage, reward savers and pensioners, and stop investors chasing risky assets just to find yield.

Low rates make borrowing affordable for households and small businesses, fund long-term investment, and widen access to finance. They can ease supply-side inflation by enabling investment, and they protect developing countries from the dollar-debt squeeze and the financial panics that sharp hikes can trigger.
