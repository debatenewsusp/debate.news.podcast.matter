Episode 126. South Sudan.
Today: the world's youngest country and its fragile peace.

South Sudan became independent in 2011. It has sixty-four ethnic groups, the largest being the Dinka, about a third of the population, and the Nuer, about a sixth, with long-running violence over land and cattle.

First, the civil war. President Salva Kiir, a Dinka and former independence fighter, accused his Nuer vice president, Riek Machar, of plotting a coup in December 2013. Machar denied it and led a rebel movement, the SPLM in Opposition, against the government's SPLM, triggering civil war. The UN sent peacekeepers, and regional mediators, along with the African Union, China, the U.S., Britain and Norway, brokered a 2015 deal that returned Machar as vice president. Fighting soon resumed, Kiir replaced Machar with another opposition figure, and both rebels and the government's Dinka base splintered.

Second, the peace. A 2018 agreement created power sharing, and Kiir and Machar formed a unity government in 2020. But it is fragile: the two men who started the war remain rivals, and in the south Thomas Cirillo's National Salvation Front continues an armed insurgency.

Since the file was written: elections have been repeatedly postponed, and in 2025 Machar was arrested and charged with treason, raising fears of a return to full war.
