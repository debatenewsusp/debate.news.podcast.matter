Episode 92. Competitive Authoritarianism.
Today: what competitive authoritarianism is, and what makes such regimes survive or fall.

First, the concept, from political scientist Steven Levitsky: regimes with real democratic institutions and elections, but a playing field tilted against the opposition through control of media, harassment of critics and sometimes election meddling. Many emerged in the nineties in Africa, Latin America and the former Soviet bloc, democratizing on the surface to please the West while relying on bribery, co-optation and quiet persecution.

Second, structure. Single-party regimes last longer than military or personalist ones, because the party resolves elite disputes. Milošević's personalist regime in Serbia collapsed as elites defected. Strong, organized parties help too: Mozambique's FRELIMO survived economic crises, while Benin's regime crumbled by 1990. But there are exceptions: Putin's personalist rule endures, and Cambodia's ruling party lasted despite weak organization.

Third, ideological cohesion. A shared ideology makes defection less attractive, and defections are dangerous because they help the opposition and show voters change is possible. Regimes born from revolution survive about three times longer than others. Ideology also builds popular support in hard times, like Vietnam's Communist Party drawing on nationalism, while Croatia's transition was helped by divisions over past war abuses.

Fourth, economic shocks. Since these regimes must look democratic, they rely on patronage and co-optation, like Mexico's PRI funding welfare programs, which costs money. Crises drain those resources: the peso crisis helped Vicente Fox end the PRI's rule in 2000, and Kenya's recession under structural adjustment pushed its ruling party into competitive elections. But hardship alone is not enough, as Orbán's Hungary shows, and the next regime is not always democratic.

Finally, backsliding. Many such regimes grew out of weak democracies, as disappointment with reformers let strongmen return, as in parts of the Western Balkans in the late 2000s. Trajectories are not linear. Democratization succeeds more where Western ties are strong, like Croatia's EU membership, where institutions hold leaders accountable, and where ethnic nationalism is not inflaming polarization.
