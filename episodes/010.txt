Episode 10. Leadership Decapitation.
Two sides today: against killing enemy leaders, and for it.

First, against decapitation.

It fails in practice. After the U.S. killed al-Zarqawi in 2006, al-Qaeda bombed the Al-Askari Shrine and Iraq saw its bloodiest years of sectarian violence. After Israel killed Hamas founder Ahmed Yassin in 2004, his successor ordered new bombings and support for Hamas surged.

It cannot be done strategically. Politicians face huge pressure to kill famous enemies after major attacks, agencies compete to look successful, bounties reward strikes regardless of strategy, and intelligence on leaders who constantly move is poor. Generals are on the clock, so there is no time to weigh costs and benefits.

It is ineffective. Groups plan for succession: Zawahiri replaced bin Laden almost immediately. Most groups run on ideology, not personality, and they institutionalize charisma through manifestos, symbols and slogans. Ironically, decapitation works best against young groups without strong institutions, but those are rarely the targets. Manhunts also cost years, like the two decades it took to catch Carlos the Jackal.

It makes terrorism worse. Weakened groups switch from hard military targets to soft civilian ones. Dead leaders become martyrs and recruiting tools. Organizations splinter, and rival cells and new leaders compete through ever more extreme violence. Negotiating with many fragmented cells is far harder than with one leader who can deliver concessions. And targeted killings may be extrajudicial and illegal.

Now, for decapitation.

Governments can use it selectively, as one tool among many, and pair the threat of assassination with incentives to negotiate. Some organizations really do depend on uniquely skilled leaders, so removing a few can turn the tide. Killing leaders shatters the image of an all-powerful group, makes broad coalitions harder to hold together, scares leadership into talks and encourages foot soldiers to defect. And the alternatives, like full invasions or indiscriminate bombing, endanger far more civilians.

Key examples. The U.S. killed Zarqawi in 2006, bin Laden in 2011, al-Shabaab's leader Godane in 2014, and Qasem Soleimani in 2020, after which Iran struck U.S. bases. Baghdadi died during a 2019 raid. Critics note that U.S. drone campaigns kill many children and that only around eight percent of victims are senior leaders. Israel has killed multiple Hamas leaders and is linked to the killing of Iranian nuclear scientists. And authoritarian states use assassination against dissidents: North Korea killed Kim Jong-nam, and Russia has been tied to the deaths or poisonings of Litvinenko, Politkovskaya, Nemtsov and Navalny.
