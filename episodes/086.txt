Episode 86. Dominant Political Parties.
Today: where dominant parties exist, how they keep power, and the role of ethnicity.

First, examples: South Africa's ANC, Turkey's AKP, India's BJP, Angola's MPLA, Uganda's NRM, and historically Japan's Liberal Democrats and Taiwan's Kuomintang. Three patterns stand out. They are mostly in post-colonial developing countries, where colonial divide-and-rule often stoked ethnic tensions. They usually led independence or liberation struggles, like the ANC against apartheid or the MPLA against Portugal, and no rival can claim to have freed the nation. And dominance is not permanent: the KMT and Japan's LDP lost power, Congress lost India to the BJP, and since the file was written, the ANC lost its majority in 2024 and had to form a coalition, the BJP lost its outright majority the same year, and Bangladesh's Awami League government was toppled by protests.

Second, how they stay in power. There is a spectrum. At one end is illiberal dominance: United Russia persecuting critics like Alexei Navalny, who died in prison in 2024, ZANU-PF's electoral fraud in Zimbabwe, Fidesz weakening Hungary's press and courts, or Ortega's Nicaragua. These parties are almost impossible to vote out, so debates rarely focus on them. At the other end is liberal dominance, earned through good governance. Most debates are about the middle: parties like the AKP, BJP and ANC that show authoritarian tendencies, like corruption scandals or weakening checks on power, but can still lose elections, as Erdogan's narrow fifty-two percent in 2023 showed.

Third, ethnicity. Many parties are explicitly ethnic, like the Basque Nationalist Party in Spain or the Bosniak Party of Democratic Action. Why? Winning one ethnic group delivers a big, loyal base. But ethnicity only matters politically if parties make it matter: by stoking fear of other groups, posing as champions of an oppressed people, using cultural symbols, running only co-ethnic candidates and invoking historical grievances. Once politicized, ethnicity is powerful because it is a shortcut for voters with little information, especially in new democracies, and because in patronage democracies, where the state controls many jobs, a party in power rewards its own group with public employment. It works best among poorer groups, since wealthier voters have more varied priorities. Not every dominant party is ethnic, though; some build multi-ethnic coalitions where no group is a majority.
