Episode 27. Dollar Hegemony.
Today: why the dollar dominates, and why that is harmful.

First, context. Most developing countries suffer from what economists call original sin: they cannot borrow abroad in their own currency. Investors already hold and trade in dollars and euros, it is cheaper for Western investors to stay in those currencies, and they distrust emerging-market currencies as volatile and vulnerable to populism and speculation.

The dollar dominates everywhere. About sixty percent of global reserves are in dollars, far ahead of the euro. Most trade outside Europe is invoiced in dollars, including almost all trade in the Americas. Global banking runs largely on dollars, commodities like oil are priced in dollars, and in a crisis everyone runs to dollar safe assets, helped by Federal Reserve swap lines.

Now, why dollar hegemony is bad. The likely alternative would have been the IMF's Special Drawing Rights, a more stable basket of currencies, which the U.S. blocked.

One, eurodollars. Huge amounts of dollars sit in foreign banks outside the Fed's oversight, with thinner reserves and no deposit insurance. Their dollar lending is not backed by actual dollars, so in a panic they hit a dollar shortage, which is why European banks scrambled for dollars in 2008. It also weakens the Fed's own policy tools.

Two, contagion. Because global finance runs on dollars, a U.S. shock becomes a global recession, and a stronger dollar makes oil and other inputs pricier for everyone.

Three, dollar debt. Around eighty percent of cross-border loans and two-thirds of developing-country sovereign debt are in dollars. In a crisis the dollar rises, so these debts balloon just when economies are weakest. High U.S. rates in the eighties caused Latin America's lost decade of debt crises, and in 2018 Turkey's dollar debts outgrew its reserves. Local banks that take deposits in local currency but owe dollars also face runs.

Four, the exorbitant privilege. The U.S. borrows cheaply, which lets households and firms overborrow and fuels bubbles.

Five, pegs. Dozens of countries use or peg to the dollar, so they import U.S. interest rates set for the U.S. economy, not theirs.

Finally, dollar hegemony enables U.S. neo-colonialism. After leaving the Iran nuclear deal in 2018, the U.S. cut Iran off from the dollar system, forcing even Europe to stop trading with it, and Iran's economy shrank sharply. Washington can skip multilateral negotiation entirely. And protecting the petrodollar helps explain U.S. support for Saudi Arabia and its wars in the Gulf.
