Episode 66. Development of Artificial Intelligence.
Today: the companies, how neural networks learn, how large language models work, and the risk of an AI race. This reflects the file's 2023 snapshot.

First, the players. Model developers like OpenAI, backed by Microsoft, Google DeepMind and Anthropic, along with firms applying AI to defense, data, drug discovery, security and sales. The supply chain runs from chip designers like Nvidia, to manufacturers like TSMC, to cloud providers like Amazon and Microsoft.

Second, how neural networks learn, in plain terms. Instead of programmers writing step-by-step rules, the network learns from examples. Data is turned into numbers and passed through layers of artificial neurons. Each neuron weighs its inputs, adds a bias, and produces a value between zero and one, which feeds the next layer. Early layers detect simple features, like an edge or an eye, and deeper layers combine them into concepts, like a face. Networks with several hidden layers are called deep.

Learning means adjusting the weights to reduce error. The network measures how wrong its predictions are, then uses gradient descent, like walking downhill on a landscape of errors, to tweak the weights. To save time, it learns from small random batches of data, a bit like polling a sample. Backpropagation, popularized in 1986, efficiently calculates how much each weight contributed to the error. Engineers fight overfitting, where a model memorizes noise instead of learning patterns, with tricks like penalizing large weights, randomly dropping neurons during training, and stopping early. In theory, a network can approximate almost any function, which is why it can recognize music or translate text. The downsides: it is hard to debug, and nobody fully understands what happens inside.

Third, large language models. Text is cleaned and split into tokens, often pieces of words. The model predicts the most likely next token given the context. Words are represented as vectors, so similar meanings sit close together, like king and queen. The big breakthrough was the transformer, from the 2017 paper Attention Is All You Need: attention lets the model weigh which words matter most to each other, even far apart in a sentence. Models like GPT can then handle new tasks with few or no examples; OpenAI's 2020 GPT-3 paper was titled Language Models are Few-Shot Learners. They are trained on enormous text datasets using vast computing power.

Finally, the private sector. The risk is a corporate arms race: if being first matters most, firms cut corners on safety, and careful developers lose to reckless ones, especially with weak regulation and U.S.-China rivalry. Philosopher Nick Bostrom warned that racing teams gradually lower their safety precautions. On the other hand, the risk may be overstated: companies fear reputational damage, lawsuits and regulators, and many AI researchers care deeply about safety and doing good.
