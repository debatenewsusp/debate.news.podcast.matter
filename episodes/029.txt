Episode 29. Football Leagues.
Today: how football leagues work, and who owns the clubs.

First, the leagues. Europe's fifty-five national leagues are governed by UEFA. Each country has a pyramid, with a top division like the Premier League and lower tiers, linked by promotion and relegation. UEFA also runs the Champions League, the Nations League and the European Championship, the most-watched events after the World Cup. The Big Five leagues, England, Germany, Spain, Italy and France, dominate sponsorship and TV money. Outside Europe, know Brazil's Série A, Mexico's Liga MX, America's MLS, Japan's J League, and the Saudi Pro League.

Second, money. Clubs earn from tickets, merchandise, sponsorship and broadcasting, but wages, transfer fees and stadium costs are huge, and many struggle. Leeds United overspent on stars and fell two divisions; Barcelona borrowed about one and a half billion euros to renovate the Camp Nou; Chelsea ran deficits under Abramovich. In some years half the Premier League does not break even. Yet clubs rarely die: almost all English Football League clubs have existed for a century.

Third, ownership. There are four main models.

Fan ownership: Barcelona is the classic example, along with AFC Wimbledon, FC United of Manchester, formed in protest at the Glazers, and St. Pauli, known for its left-wing fan culture.

Wealthy individuals: Sheikh Mansour at Manchester City, the Agnelli family at Juventus, Stan Kroenke at Arsenal, and formerly Abramovich at Chelsea.

States and sovereign wealth funds: Saudi Arabia's Public Investment Fund owns Newcastle, and Qatar's investment authority owns PSG. Why? Not profit: even Real Madrid earns under a billion dollars a year, tiny next to a large company. Football buys social, political and cultural capital, which critics call sportswashing.

Institutional and corporate investors: private equity, hedge funds like Elliott at AC Milan, and companies like Red Bull, betting that club values will keep rising, as American sports franchises have.
