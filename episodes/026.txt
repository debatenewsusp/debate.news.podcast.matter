Episode 26. SCOTUS on Affirmative Action.
Today: the 2023 Supreme Court ruling ending race-conscious admissions, the precedents, the reasoning, and what comes next.

First, the case. Students for Fair Admissions, a group led by activist Edward Blum and representing rejected Asian-American applicants, sued Harvard and the University of North Carolina. They argued that considering race violated the Civil Rights Act and the Fourteenth Amendment's Equal Protection Clause. The Court agreed, six to three for UNC and six to two for Harvard.

Second, the precedents. Bakke in 1978 banned racial quotas but allowed race as one factor among many. Adarand in 1995 required strict scrutiny: a compelling interest, narrowly tailored. Grutter in 2003 accepted diversity as a compelling interest, but said racial preferences should no longer be needed in twenty-five years.

Third, Chief Justice Roberts's reasoning. One, the goals, like training future leaders or building empathy, are too vague to measure or review, and there is no end date. Two, the means do not match the goals: the categories are crude, lumping together South Asian and East Asian students, for example. Three, the programs break two rules: race may never be a negative, and it may never be a stereotype. Admissions are zero-sum, so a boost for some is a penalty for others, and lower courts found Asian-American admissions fell under Harvard's policy. Assuming people of one race think alike is demeaning. Two exceptions remain: military academies are not covered, and students can still write about how race shaped their lives in essays.

Fourth, the evidence from states that already banned affirmative action. After California's Proposition 209, underrepresented minority enrollment at Berkeley and UCLA fell sharply, and despite about half a billion dollars spent on race-neutral programs, only around three percent of Berkeley's 2022 admits were Black. Michigan saw Black undergraduate enrollment fall by more than half.

Fifth, the alternatives: class-based preferences, though simulations show racial gaps persist; automatic admission for top students in each high school, hard to apply at elite colleges; proxies like zip codes; targeted outreach and financial aid; test-optional policies; ending legacy admissions, which raised first-generation enrollment at Johns Hopkins; and, above all, the essay loophole, which Harvard quoted within hours.

Finally, the ruling may chill minority scholarships and corporate diversity programs, both through fear of lawsuits and by reinforcing the claim that racism is no longer a problem.
