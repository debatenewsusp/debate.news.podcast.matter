Episode 47. Moral Philosophy.
A toolkit episode today: the core moral clashes that come up again and again.

First, utilitarianism versus deontology. For utilitarianism: many duties, like don't lie, are really justified by consequences; if objective moral rules exist we cannot reliably know them; pleasure and pain are the only sure moral constants; and deontology assumes free will, which is doubtful. For deontology: people are not bags of chemicals on a utility spreadsheet, they have dignity; pure consequentialism can justify horrors like slavery; consequences are uncertain while intentions are knowable; and even utilitarian arguments, like animal welfare, rest on rights claims.

Second, instrumentalization is wrong. Using someone merely as a means ignores their intrinsic worth, imposes your idea of the good on them, and treats a person as an object.

Third, self-defense is just. When someone threatens your rights, they forfeit some of their own, so you may act against them.

Fourth, acts versus omissions. One side says doing harm involves intent, while failing to prevent it does not. The other says choosing not to act is still a choice.

Fifth, future generations. Prioritize them: there will be far more of them, and they may be worse off. Deprioritize them: their existence and needs are uncertain, we are better equipped to solve today's problems, and sacrificing people now for them is its own instrumentalization.

Sixth, autonomy. It comes before utility, bodily autonomy needs a compelling reason to limit, and respecting choices tends to maximize preferences anyway. Bans can still be justified to protect third parties, to protect people from future harm, like seatbelt laws, or from risks they could not knowingly consent to.

Finally, free will. Against it: decisions are just neurochemistry, conditioned by past experience, and the universe is either determined or random. For it: compatibilism, the view that even in a law-governed universe, choices can still come from within us.
