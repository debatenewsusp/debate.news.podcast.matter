Episode 16. Free Speech.
Two sides today: defending free speech, and curbing it.

First, defending free speech.

Speech is tied to identity: it is how we project our values and beliefs, so restricting it restricts who we are.

It is how we find truth. Following Mill, a censored opinion might be true, and nobody is infallible enough to decide for everyone. Censorship turns ideas into dogma, and even worthless speech is useful because defending good ideas against it keeps them sharp.

Political speech is special. It drives debate about the best policies, keeps governments accountable, and makes democracy tolerable for the losing side: my vote rarely swings an election, but my voice can change minds.

Bans also backfire. The Streisand effect spreads banned content. Conspiracy theorists say elites hide the truth, so bans prove their point. Banned extremists become martyrs, as neo-Nazis did after the Skokie march ban in 1977. Pushed off mainstream platforms, they radicalize further in echo chambers, and without a safety valve they may turn to violence. The answer to bad speech is more speech.

And who decides? Hate speech and misinformation are hard to define, and ambiguity invites abuse by power-hungry governments. Draw the line broadly and good speech gets banned; draw it narrowly and bad speech escapes. Germany's NetzDG law, with heavy fines for slow removal, led platforms to over-censor, catching anti-Nazi forums and mainstream commentators. Rwanda's divisionism laws, meant to stop genocide ideology, were later used to ban the BBC over a documentary.

Now, curbing free speech.

No right is absolute. We already restrict libel, fighting words and obscenity, and limit speech by time and place. Canada's Charter guarantees expression only within reasonable limits that can be justified in a free and democratic society.

Hate speech causes real harm in three layers. First, trauma: slurs degrade people's sense of self-worth. Second, fear: hateful speech signals that humiliation could escalate to violence. Third, a chilling effect: a few hateful voices silence many vulnerable ones, so banning hate can actually protect equal speech.

The marketplace of ideas does not work. Centuries of debate have not ended racism. People are biased, distracted and drawn to style over substance. False claims are easy to make and hard to rebut, so bad speech sells well. And hosting a debate on someone's humanity implies it is up for debate.

Finally, consider juries. When ordinary people must find the truth in court, we tightly control what they hear, banning hearsay and inadmissible evidence. If society's search for truth matters as much, regulating harmful speech is justified.
