Episode 88. Court Debt.
Today: the case against court-imposed fines and fees, especially in the United States.

First, the problem. Courts increasingly impose fines on poor defendants and then jail those who cannot pay. Former prisoners owe an estimated twenty-seven billion dollars in court debt.

Second, it makes communities less safe. Ex-prisoners already struggle to find work, and debt deepens the desperation that drives people back to crime. Worse, enforcement blocks employment directly: California suspended millions of driver's licenses over unpaid debts, though many need cars to get to work, and New Jersey courts summoning debtors in the middle of the workday cost many their jobs.

Third, it isolates people. Ex-prisoners face stigma, avoid hospitals and schools for fear of arrest, often lose the right to vote, and internalize shame, which feeds addiction and mental illness.

Fourth, it deepens racial injustice. Court fees trace back to Reconstruction, when Southern courts used them to force freed Black people into bonded labor. Today, research finds Black men carry court debts more than double their expected earnings, far worse than white men, and suffer worse health effects.

Fifth, it is unjust. People who served their time get punished twice. And fines ignore ability to pay, so the same offense punishes the poor far more harshly, violating proportionality.

Sixth, the revenue argument fails. Supporters say fines fund courts, but collection is expensive: jailing defaulters and paying probation officers costs so much that one New Mexico county spent more than a dollar to collect each dollar. And recidivism costs far more.

Finally, reform: scale fines to ability to pay, stop jailing people for debt, stop suspending licenses, and allow courts to waive old debts, as Ohio did, which helped reduce reoffending.
