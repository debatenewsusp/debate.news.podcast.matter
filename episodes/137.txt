Episode 137. Myanmar.
Today: Myanmar's history and ethnic wars, the 2021 coup and resistance, the regional fallout, and how China and ASEAN have responded.

First, history. Burma's kingdoms were weakened by the eighteenth century, and Britain conquered it in three wars, completing the takeover in 1885, fearing threats to British India and French rivals and eyeing its gems and rice. Colonial rule was harsh: villages were razed to crush rebellions, and farmers fell into debt to moneylenders. Nationalists organized through Buddhist associations and student strikes. Aung San allied with Japan to expel Britain, then switched sides when Japan proved a new occupier, and negotiated independence, promising autonomy to ethnic minorities in the Panglong Agreement. He was assassinated in 1947, the Bamar majority backed away from those promises, and civil war broke out in 1948. It has never fully stopped. In 1962 General Ne Win seized power, imposing a one-party military state, Bamar nationalism and economic isolation.

Second, the ethnic armed organizations. Myanmar recognizes a hundred and thirty-five ethnic groups, with a Bamar Buddhist majority. The Karen, about seven percent, have fought for independence since 1949, weakened by the army's Four Cuts strategy of cutting rebels off from food, funds, information and recruits. The mostly Christian Kachin fight religious persecution and Chinese-backed projects like the Myitsone dam. The Shan, about a tenth, have long been tied to the opium trade. The United Wa State Army runs a de facto autonomous state tied to drug trafficking and close to China. And the Muslim Rohingya of Rakhine State were stripped of citizenship in 1982 and suffered genocide, especially from 2016, fueled by extremist Buddhist nationalism.

Third, democratization and the coup. In the 1988 uprising, Aung San Suu Kyi emerged as democracy's leader; her National League for Democracy won the 1990 election, but the military ignored it and arrested her. After monk-led protests in 2007, the Saffron Revolution, the junta wrote a constitution reserving a quarter of parliamentary seats for the military, and the NLD won in 2015. In November 2020 it won again in a landslide. The military alleged fraud, and on February 1, 2021, it seized power under General Min Aung Hlaing, arrested Suu Kyi and cracked down on mass protests, killing hundreds.

Fourth, the resistance. The exiled National Unity Government organized People's Defense Forces, poorly armed with homemade weapons and funded by the diaspora. The ethnic armies are stronger, with decades of experience, but no unified federal army exists because of distrust between ethnic groups and the Bamar, disagreement over the future and no single leader. In October 2023, three groups known as the Brotherhood Alliance launched Operation 1027 in the north, overrunning hundreds of army posts. The junta tries to split ethnic armies from the NUG with airstrikes, while its brutality drives recruits to the rebels.

Fifth, regional fallout. Myanmar is the world's top opium producer, refugees, especially Rohingya, have fled to Bangladesh and beyond, fighting spills into China, Thailand and India, and the junta revived ties with North Korea.

Sixth, the great powers. Russia is the junta's main arms supplier, from fighter jets to drones. China is more cautious: it invests in an economic corridor of railways and pipelines from the Bay of Bengal, which helps solve its Malacca Dilemma, the fear that a U.S. or Indian blockade of the narrow Strait of Malacca could choke its oil imports. But Beijing also keeps ties to ethnic armies on its border and pushes for talks. Its support is about interests, not ideology. The U.S. passed the BURMA Act in 2022, authorizing non-lethal help to the resistance.

Seventh, ASEAN. Its 2021 five-point consensus demanded an end to violence, dialogue, an envoy and aid, but the junta ignored it, even executing four activists in 2022 after warnings. Myanmar's leaders were barred from summits. Members split: Thailand and Cambodia favored engagement, Malaysia and Brunei pushed hard against the junta, Indonesia preferred quiet diplomacy, and Singapore criticized the junta while firms based there supplied it with arms. ASEAN's principles of non-interference and consensus give every member a veto, undermining its claim to centrality.

Eighth, the economy. Poverty has doubled since the coup, the military profits through its own conglomerates and state gas revenues, and sanctions have deepened cronyism and informality.

Since the file was written: rebels made big gains in 2024, though Chinese pressure forced some to return captured cities, a huge earthquake struck central Myanmar in 2025, and the junta held widely dismissed elections from December 2025 that kept the military in charge.

That was Myanmar. Next episode: Cambodia.
