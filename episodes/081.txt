Episode 81. Underprivileged Schools.
Today: why education matters, why poor schools are underfunded, why funding matters, and extra barriers in developing countries.

First, why it matters. Education tracks with longer lives, better health and higher incomes, through knowledge, soft skills and hard skills. It also brings less crime, more growth, fewer deaths of despair, higher voter turnout and stronger social cohesion. UNESCO estimates universal basic literacy would lift well over a hundred million people out of extreme poverty.

Second, funding gaps. Where schools are funded locally, like through property taxes in the U.S., rich districts outspend poor ones and inequality perpetuates itself, as in struggling districts like Hartford, Connecticut. Where funding is national, poor districts lack political clout, education gets cut first under austerity, wealthy parents opt for private schools, and politicians avoid long-term investments. Even in Europe, students in the poorest neighborhoods rarely reach top PISA scores, and Dutch schools are more segregated than in 2000.

Third, money matters. Higher spending per pupil correlates with better results, even if not spent perfectly: more and better teachers, smaller classes, materials, free lunches, early childhood education and competitive pay that reduces burnout. Vermont's 1997 school finance reform raised scores in poorer districts, and a Kansas funding increase boosted college enrollment.

Fourth, teaching. Poor districts cannot pay competitive wages, so they hire less qualified teachers who often leave quickly. Wealthier students arrive better prepared, with tutors, parents who read to them, and know-how about seeking help and applying for programs.

Fifth, barriers in poorer countries. Sanitation: without clean, private toilets, girls miss school during their periods; studies in Kenya, South Sudan and India find large shares of girls staying home. Harvest seasons: in Bangladesh, exams set during the rice harvest push dropout up. Child marriage: girls who marry young usually leave school, and the Malala Fund estimates twelve years of schooling for every girl would cut child marriage by nearly two-thirds. Disasters, wars and pandemics, like Syria's civil war or Ebola in Liberia, disrupt schooling. And IMF austerity has pushed cuts to public wages and education budgets, as in Zambia and Nepal.
