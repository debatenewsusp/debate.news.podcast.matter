Episode 115. Yemen.
Today: Yemen's long history of fragmentation, the two Yemens, unification, the rise of the Houthis and the civil war.

First, fragmentation. For a thousand years, Zaidi Shia imams ruled the northern highlands, but Yemen never had a strong central state. The Ottomans took it in the sixteenth century to protect Mecca and Indian Ocean trade, but Zaidi rebels expelled them by 1638, and Yemen prospered by monopolizing coffee. By the nineteenth century the state crumbled, Britain seized the port of Aden, and the Ottomans returned to the center.

Second, two Yemens. After World War One, the northern imams formed a kingdom, weakened by a war with Saudi Arabia and a paranoid ruler. In 1962 Egyptian-backed nationalists overthrew the new imam, and an eight-year civil war, with Egypt, Saudi Arabia and the Cold War superpowers involved, ended with the Yemen Arab Republic in the north. In the south, Marxist and Arab nationalist rebels drove Britain out of Aden in 1967, creating a Marxist one-party state, the People's Democratic Republic of Yemen. The two were mostly friendly and unified in 1990 as the Soviet bloc collapsed.

Third, unification's problems. The state was weak, ruled through patronage, military elites and Saudi-backed sheikhs. Southerners felt marginalized, and in 1994 they tried to secede, but lost a short civil war. President Ali Abdullah Saleh stayed in power until 2012, and southern separatism lived on as the Southern Movement.

Fourth, the Houthis, formally Ansar Allah. Zaidi Shia in the north resented Saudi-backed Sunni influence and Saleh's ties to the West, especially after the 2003 Iraq invasion, adopting slogans like Death to America. In 2004 Saleh tried to arrest their leader Hussein al-Houthi, who was killed, sparking years of insurgency, Saudi involvement, and the arrival of al-Qaeda in the Arabian Peninsula and alleged Iranian help.

Fifth, the uprising. During the 2011 Arab Spring, protests over unemployment and corruption spread, a powerful tribe turned against Saleh, and his palace was shelled. A Gulf-brokered deal gave him immunity and handed power to his deputy, Abdrabbuh Mansur Hadi, who ran unopposed, angering the Houthis, who seized more northern territory and won some Sunni support.

Finally, war. In 2014, after fuel subsidy cuts sparked protests, the Houthis stormed the capital Sanaa and forced the government out. It became a proxy war: a Saudi-led coalition backed by the West supported Hadi, while Iran backed the Houthis, causing one of the world's worst humanitarian crises, and southern separatists formed the Southern Transitional Council. Since the file was written, the Houthis have attacked Red Sea shipping since late 2023 in the name of Gaza, prompting U.S. and British strikes.

That was Yemen. Next episode: Syria.
