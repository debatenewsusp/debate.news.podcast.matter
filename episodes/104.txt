Episode 104. China's History.
Today: from the fall of the Qing to Mao and Deng, and then the Century of Humiliation that shapes the Communist Party's story.

First, the end of empire. By the nineteenth century the Qing dynasty was weakened by internal divisions and foreign powers. The anti-foreign Boxer Rebellion failed, late reforms satisfied no one, and the 1911 Xinhai Revolution ended imperial rule. After Yuan Shikai died in 1916, China fractured into the Warlord Era. The Nationalists, the KMT, and the Communists, the CCP, founded in 1921, briefly allied, until the KMT purged the Communists in 1927 and civil war began. They paused to fight Japan in World War Two, then resumed, and in 1949 Mao's Communists won and the Nationalists fled to Taiwan.

Second, Mao. He built a cult of personality and ruled through waves of purges against capitalists, intellectuals, rightists and suspected enemies. The Great Leap Forward from 1958 forced collectivized farming and rapid industrialization and caused one of the deadliest famines in history, with tens of millions dead. Why it failed: Mao pushed impossible quotas partly to prove China's communism purer than Khrushchev's Soviet Union; terrified local officials faked output, so planners raised targets further; pay ignored effort; backyard furnaces made useless iron; and pseudo-scientific campaigns, like killing sparrows, unleashed locust plagues, while men were sent to steel works and farms were left to women, children and the elderly. Then in 1966 came the Cultural Revolution, meant to restore Mao's control and destroy the Four Olds: old customs, culture, habits and ideas. Red Guards attacked intellectuals, leaders like President Liu Shaoqi were purged, cultural heritage was destroyed, and millions of urban youth were sent to the countryside.

Third, Deng Xiaoping. Himself a victim of Mao's purges, Deng put pragmatism over ideology, inspired partly by Singapore's technocracy. He decollectivized farming, let in foreign capital and private business, lifted price controls and created special economic zones like Shenzhen, under the label socialism with Chinese characteristics, producing growth often above nine percent. He restored order and the university entrance exam, enforced the one-child policy, normalized relations with the U.S., and proposed One Country, Two Systems for Hong Kong and Macau. But his reforms widened inequality, angered Maoists, and he oversaw the bloody Tiananmen Square crackdown in 1989.

Fourth, the Century of Humiliation, from 1839 to 1949. China had long dominated East Asia through culture, trade and the Silk Road. Then Britain, exporting opium to fix its trade deficit, went to war when China destroyed opium stocks, and the 1842 Treaty of Nanking ceded Hong Kong and opened China's ports. The Second Opium War saw British and French troops sack the imperial gardens near Beijing; Russia took northern territory; France took influence over Vietnam; Japan, modernized by the Meiji reforms, crushed China in 1894 and took Taiwan and control over Korea. Then came foreign suppression of the Boxers, Japan's Twenty-One Demands, Japan seizing Manchuria in 1931 and invading China in 1937, with atrocities like the Nanjing Massacre.

This is the Communist Party's founding narrative: loss and redemption. Proclaiming the People's Republic in 1949, Mao declared that China, once great, had fallen behind only because of foreign imperialism and corrupt governments, and that it had now stood up and would never again be humiliated. That story runs through Chinese propaganda and education today.
