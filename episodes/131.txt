Episode 131. Jamaica.
Today: gang violence and politics in Jamaica.

First, the scale. At independence in 1962, Jamaica had one of the world's lowest murder rates, about four per hundred thousand. It rose to among the highest, near sixty. Jamaican ports are transit points for cocaine and arms, funding gangs known as posses, like the Shower Posse and the Klansman gang, often backed by financiers in London, Toronto and New York.

Second, politics and gangs. Both major parties, the Jamaica Labour Party and the People's National Party, have links to gangs. Politicians have given dons weapons and protection in exchange for votes and voter intimidation, creating garrison communities where gang bosses also provide social support. When the U.S. sought the extradition of Shower Posse leader Christopher Coke, Prime Minister Bruce Golding hired lawyers to resist, because Coke's network delivered votes.

Third, causes. Poverty, poor schooling, and the pandemic drove recruitment, and almost half of children grow up in single-parent homes, leading some young men to seek belonging in gangs. Most of the roughly two hundred and seventy gangs are loose and small, funded by lottery scams and extortion. Justice is weak: police bribery scandals, frequent jailbreaks, blackmailed judges, frightened witnesses and political interference mean few convictions.

Fourth, states of emergency. In late 2022, Prime Minister Andrew Holness declared emergencies allowing searches and detentions without warrants. Courts had struck down earlier ones and activists warn of abuse; the Senate refused to extend one, so he declared a new one in nine of fourteen parishes, including tourist hubs like Montego Bay. It is a debate about security versus rights.

Since the file was written: murders fell sharply in 2024 and 2025, which the government credits to its crackdown, and Holness won a third term in 2025.
