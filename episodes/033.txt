Episode 33. al-Qaeda.
Today: 9/11, al-Qaeda's origins, the CIA's torture program, and Guantanamo.

First, the attacks. On September 11, 2001, hijacked planes hit both World Trade Center towers and the Pentagon, while passengers on United 93 fought back and crashed it in Pennsylvania. The towers collapsed and nearly three thousand people died.

Second, origins. Osama bin Laden, a wealthy Saudi who had funded and fought with the Afghan mujahideen against the Soviets, founded al-Qaeda in Peshawar, Pakistan, in 1988, helped by Saudi-funded Wahhabi religious schools. It embraced violent jihad against both apostate Arab regimes and Western, especially American, forces in the Muslim world. Bin Laden called Americans legitimate targets, civilian or military, citing U.S. troops in Saudi Arabia and support for violence against Muslims in Palestine, Bosnia, Chechnya and Kashmir. In 1998 he declared war on the U.S., and that August al-Qaeda bombed U.S. embassies in Kenya and Tanzania, killing over two hundred. Khalid Sheikh Mohammed then masterminded 9/11 from abroad, with Mohamed Atta leading the hijackers in America.

Third, enhanced interrogation. Days after 9/11, Bush gave the CIA broad power to capture and detain suspects, and Congress passed the Authorization for Use of Military Force. Fearing hardened operatives would not talk, the CIA paid two psychologists millions to design "enhanced interrogation techniques," built on the idea of learned helplessness: slapping, walling, stress positions, cramped confinement, sleep deprivation for over a week, and waterboarding. Abu Zubaydah was waterboarded over eighty times at a black site in Thailand, and Khalid Sheikh Mohammed one hundred and eighty-three times. Lawyers, including John Yoo's torture memos, argued this was legal unless it caused organ failure or death. Of over a hundred known detainees, at least one died and over two dozen were wrongly held. Abu Ghraib caused global outrage in 2004, and Obama ended the program in 2009.

Finally, Guantanamo. The U.S. has leased the base from Cuba since 1903, and Cuba calls it illegal. Because it is not U.S. soil, detainees had minimal constitutional protections, though the Supreme Court ruled in 2006 that the Geneva Conventions still applied. Around seven hundred and eighty people have passed through, leaked files showed many were innocent, and a few dozen remain, many never tried. Obama and Biden promised closure, but Congress blocked it.
