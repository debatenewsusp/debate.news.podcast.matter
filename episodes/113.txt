Episode 113. Iran.
Today: Iran's history with the West, the nuclear deal and the debate over it, the Mahsa Amini protests, and what has happened since.

First, history. In 1953 the CIA and Britain's MI6 helped overthrow Iran's elected prime minister after he nationalized a British oil company, restoring the Shah, whose rule brought growth but also a feared secret police. The 1979 revolution created an anti-Western theocracy, and the U.S. embassy hostage crisis triggered sanctions. More followed in 1987 over support for terrorism, since Iran funds Hezbollah with hundreds of millions a year, plus the Houthis and Hamas, and in 2006 when the UN sanctioned Iran's uranium enrichment. Sanctions, including U.S. secondary sanctions that punish anyone trading with Iran, crushed its currency and economy, and pushed it to negotiate.

Second, the nuclear deal, the JCPOA, signed in 2015 with the U.S., U.K., France, Russia, China and Germany: sanctions relief in exchange for limits on centrifuges and enriched uranium stockpiles plus inspections by the IAEA, stretching Iran's breakout time to a bomb from two or three months to about a year. In 2018 Trump withdrew and reimposed sanctions, Europe stayed in but feared U.S. penalties, Iran began exceeding the limits in 2019, and in 2021 the hardliner Ebrahim Raisi became president. Iran distrusted any new deal, fearing the U.S. would walk away again, and hardliners resisted because state-linked firms controlling much of the economy benefit from isolation.

Third, the debate. Against the deal: sanctions relief frees billions for militias like the Houthis and Hezbollah, Israel strongly opposed it, it ignored ballistic missiles, which Iran calls non-negotiable since its air force is outdated and it remembers Iraqi missile attacks, its limits expire after fifteen years, and lifting sanctions throws away leverage. For the deal: after sanctions were lifted in 2016 Iran's economy grew strongly; sanctions cause currency collapse, inflation and falling wages and push Iran toward China; and the restrictions and inspections make a bomb harder, while no deal risks an arms race including Saudi Arabia.

Fourth, Mahsa Amini. In September 2022 the morality police detained the twenty-two-year-old Kurdish woman for improper hijab, and she died in custody. Protests under the slogan Woman, Life, Freedom became the biggest challenge to the regime in decades. Unlike the 2009 Green Movement or the 2019 fuel protests, they united men and women, cities and countryside, all classes, spread through social media, included nationwide strikes and reached conservative cities like Qom. The West sanctioned officials, the UN opened an investigation, and the regime responded with brutal force, including executions.

Since the file was written: Raisi died in a helicopter crash in 2024 and the reformist Masoud Pezeshkian was elected. In 2025, Israel and then the U.S. struck Iran's nuclear sites, and UN sanctions were reimposed. In early 2026, a wider U.S.-Israeli war killed Supreme Leader Ali Khamenei, and his son Mojtaba was named successor. The situation is still evolving.

That was Iran. Next episode: Armenia and Azerbaijan.
