Episode 41. AI in Law Enforcement.
Today: facial recognition, predictive policing, and other uses of AI in criminal justice.

First, facial recognition. Clearview AI scraped tens of billions of photos from social media and has been searched over a million times by U.S. police. Several cities and countries, including Canada, Australia, Britain and France, have restricted or banned it over privacy, and the EU has issued large fines.

The case against: it can supercharge racial profiling. Development teams are not diverse, training data overrepresents white faces in stock photos and Black faces in mugshots, and it struggles with young people whose faces change. Officers overtrust it as an objective shortcut and use it to confirm their own biases.

The case for: it analyzes patterns beyond human ability, processes hours of footage in seconds, does not get tired, reconstructs blurry images and grainy CCTV, and is improving at reading whole scenes. It helped capture an MS-13 leader in 2019 after an eight-year manhunt.

Second, predictive policing, which uses past crime data to send officers to likely hotspots. The best-known tool, PredPol, has been criticized for bias, sending police disproportionately to Black, Latino and low-income neighborhoods. Models are only as good as their data, and crime data is full of errors, underreported crimes like sexual assault, manipulated statistics and biased past arrests. Fixing this is hard: vendors will not admit flaws, enthusiasm crowds out criticism, unions resist paperwork, counterfactuals are nearly impossible to build, and data is often classified. The result is a self-fulfilling prophecy: we looked for crime, we found it, and racial profiling gets a scientific cover.

The defense: AI makes predictions far more precise, using variables humans ignore, like lighting and temperature, which strengthens deterrence. Social network analysis can map gang ties, as Chicago police did with social media data, after which violence dropped. AI also lacks the deliberate prejudice of racist officers, and data bias can be reduced through audits, using 911 calls rather than arrests, and pooling data across cities.

Finally, other uses: traffic management, fraud detection, forensic science and DNA analysis, and legal work like summarizing statutes and speeding up evidence review.
