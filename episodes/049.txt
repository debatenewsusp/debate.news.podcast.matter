Episode 49. Higher Education.
Today: access to university, the economics of higher education, why universities research, and their social role.

First, the facts. Most of the world gets primary education, but fewer than forty percent enroll in university. In rich countries, women now graduate at much higher rates than men.

Second, barriers to access. Cost: tuition and living expenses stay high even where tuition is subsidized, and in sub-Saharan Africa less than a tenth of young adults can afford college; poor students also give up wages to study. Weak schools: underfunded schools leave students unprepared, lowering admission and raising dropout. Geography: top universities sit in cities or wealthy suburbs, far from rural students, who often face culture shock. Discrimination: women are effectively barred in places like Afghanistan, and minorities like India's Muslims are badly underrepresented. And overcrowded public universities simply turn people away.

Third, the economics. Students study to learn for its own sake and to invest in skills. Degrees raise productivity and signal competence to employers. Governments fund higher education because it creates broader benefits, like stronger families and more productive workers; because private student lending fails, since you cannot repossess knowledge, banks cannot judge returns, and the riskiest borrowers borrow most; and because a degree is a risky upfront bet. Funding can go to students, through scholarships and cheap loans, or to universities, sometimes based on performance, as in the Netherlands.

Fourth, research. Universities pair teaching and research because it is efficient, central to their mission, attracts top students and staff, brings in grants and industry partnerships, and professors care about it. Research advances medicine, technology and social policy, knowledge is valuable for its own sake, and students learn at the frontier. Most funding comes from outside, like government science agencies and private foundations.

Fifth, social good. Universities are engines of social change: Myanmar's 1988 democracy movement began with students in Yangon, Brazil's national student union has pushed reforms, Chilean students led protests against inequality in 2011, and students helped drive decolonization across Africa. University clinics also serve local communities.
