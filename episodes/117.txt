Episode 117. Afghanistan.
Today: from the Soviet war to the Taliban, twenty years of U.S. occupation, the 2021 collapse, and life under Taliban rule.

First, background. Afghanistan is mostly Sunni, ethnically diverse, with Pashtuns the largest group. After a monarchy ended in the seventies, a 1978 communist coup brought the Soviet-backed PDPA to power, and in 1979 the Soviets invaded to save it. The mujahideen, a diverse coalition armed by the U.S., Pakistan and Saudi Arabia, fought them until the Soviets left in 1989. The communist government fell in 1992, and the mujahideen turned on each other.

Second, the Taliban. Formed by refugees schooled in Pakistani madrassas and disillusioned fighters, it swept the country from 1994, ruling most of it by 1996 with extreme repression, especially of women, backed by Pakistan. Two days before 9/11, al-Qaeda assassinated Ahmad Shah Massoud, leader of the anti-Taliban Northern Alliance.

Third, the occupation. When the Taliban refused to hand over bin Laden, the U.S. invaded in October 2001 and took Kabul within weeks. The Bonn Agreement created an interim government under Hamid Karzai, protected by the NATO-led ISAF. Reconstruction teams tried to rebuild, criticized as disorganized. The Taliban insurgency revived from 2003, elections in 2004 and 2005 brought millions of voters including many women, but the 2009 vote was marred by fraud. Obama surged troops, bin Laden was killed in 2011, and security was handed to Afghan forces by 2014.

Fourth, the peace process. In February 2020, the Trump administration signed the Doha Agreement: a U.S. withdrawal in exchange for Taliban promises to block terrorist groups and negotiate with Kabul, which was excluded from the talks and forced to release thousands of Taliban prisoners. Biden set a withdrawal deadline for September 2021.

Fifth, the collapse. The Taliban seized district after district, and in August 2021 Kabul fell within days, far faster than predicted, as President Ashraf Ghani fled and a chaotic airport evacuation followed. Why? Intelligence failures. Afghan forces, despite tens of billions in U.S. training, often surrendered or cut deals, lacking faith in Kabul and demoralized by the withdrawal. They depended on Western air power and logistics. Corruption left soldiers unpaid, some selling weapons to the Taliban. And the strategy of a strong central government never fit a diverse, decentralized country.

Sixth, Taliban rule. Despite promises of moderation, the regime under reclusive leader Hibatullah Akhundzada bars girls and women from secondary school and university, favors Pashtuns and carries out public punishments. The economy collapsed: foreign aid had funded about three-quarters of public spending, and the U.S. froze billions in central bank reserves, as the IMF and World Bank halted funds. GDP fell about a fifth in 2021, and most of the population faces food insecurity, worsened by drought and floods. Aid now flows through UN agencies and NGOs. A small resistance front fights in the Panjshir Valley.

Finally, neighbors. Pakistan backed the Taliban but faces attacks from the Pakistani Taliban based there. Iran is hostile, remembering the killing of its diplomats in 1998. China seeks counterterrorism cooperation against Uyghur militants and investment. Since the file was written, Russia in 2025 became the first country to formally recognize the Taliban government.

That was Afghanistan. Next episode: Ukraine.
