Episode 84. Working From Home.
Today: how remote work spread, why the U.S. leads, the economic pros and cons, and the social effects.

First, the shift. Before COVID, only about seven percent of work was done from home. Video calls, cloud files and broadband made the pandemic shift possible, and it stuck: in rich countries people now spend around a third of their working hours at home, often in hybrid arrangements. Remote-friendly jobs tend to be better paid and better educated.

Second, why the U.S. leads: bigger homes with room for an office, many jobs in tech, finance and business services, a weaker COVID response that kept people home longer, and strong management practices that make it easier to measure performance remotely.

Third, the economic upsides. Firms can hire cheaper workers in other regions, and workers stay longer because they value flexibility. Labor pools widen: no geographic limits, no commutes, and access for parents, people with disabilities and those without transport. Matching between workers and jobs improves. Productivity can rise thanks to comfort, quiet and time saved, and companies save on expensive offices.

Fourth, the downsides. It can depress wages. Teamwork is harder and people silo themselves. Distractions at home, the bed, the fridge and the TV, can hurt productivity. Mentoring suffers without informal chats and after-work drinks, and new ideas often come from chance encounters. Hybrid work tries to balance this, with solo tasks at home and group work in person.

Fifth, wider effects. Economists warned of an office real estate apocalypse as demand for city offices falls and people move to outer suburbs, hurting property and financial markets. Women may benefit from balancing unequal domestic burdens with careers, and skipping the commute helps mental health. But fewer random encounters with colleagues who think differently may deepen political polarization.
