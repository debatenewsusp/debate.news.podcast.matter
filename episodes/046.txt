Episode 46. Monopolies.
Today: examples of monopolies, why they are bad, and why they might be good.

First, examples. Private monopolies include Standard Oil, broken up in 1911; Microsoft's dominance of software in the nineties, which led to a federal lawsuit; the British East India Company; De Beers in diamonds; United Fruit in bananas; and the Visa and Mastercard duopoly. Natural monopolies, like railways, utilities, postal services and airports, are often state-owned, as are many oil companies, like Saudi Aramco, Petrobras and Gazprom, and broadcasters, like the BBC, China's CCTV and Russia's RT.

Second, why monopolies are bad. They raise prices, cut quality and limit choice and output. Their scale creates barriers to entry that kill start-ups and reduce innovation. They entrench themselves by lobbying, capturing regulators, swamping rivals in litigation and patents, and controlling supply chains and distribution. As dominant employers they suppress wages, weaken unions and deepen inequality. They become too big to fail, winning subsidies and bailouts that encourage risk-taking. And they grow inefficient, with organizational slack, bureaucracy and inertia.

Third, why monopolies can be good. Scale lowers costs: they integrate supply chains, stabilize input prices, build long-term supplier relationships and borrow cheaply. They still face pressure to keep prices down, from media scrutiny, fear of antitrust action, substitute products, internal competition and the threat of well-funded new entrants. They can innovate more, with cheap capital for research, prestige that attracts talent, the ability to absorb losses on risky bets, and no fear of short-term bankruptcy. Start-ups benefit when big firms buy them and bring their ideas to market, and the hope of being acquired encourages entrepreneurship. Monopolies also create valuable networks, like Apple's App Store. Many became dominant precisely because they were the most innovative. And as long as the market stays contestable, so rivals could enter quickly, one dominant firm is not a problem.
