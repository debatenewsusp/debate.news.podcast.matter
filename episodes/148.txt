Episode 148. ASEAN.
Today, the final episode: what ASEAN is, how the ASEAN Way works, economic and security integration, the South China Sea, and democratic backsliding.

First, why it matters. Over six hundred million people live in ASEAN countries, trillions of dollars of trade cross their waters, and together they form one of the world's largest economies, growing about five percent a year for two decades, with fast-growing middle classes.

Second, origins. Colonial powers reshaped the region around extracting rubber, tin and oil and used divide and rule. After independence, from the Philippines in 1946 to Brunei in 1984, countries faced communist insurgencies, border disputes, great power meddling and poverty, and many fell under military or one-party rule. The Vietnam War warned them against aligning with a superpower. Earlier regional efforts failed, from the U.S.-led SEATO to the short-lived Association of Southeast Asia. In 1967, Indonesia, Malaysia, the Philippines, Singapore and Thailand signed the Bangkok Declaration, bound partly by anti-communism. The 1976 Treaty of Amity and Cooperation set the core principles: sovereignty, freedom from outside interference, non-interference in each other's affairs, peaceful settlement of disputes, renouncing force, and cooperation. A 1995 treaty made the region a nuclear-weapon-free zone.

Third, the ASEAN Way: informal, consensus-based diplomacy and non-interference. It accommodates huge diversity, but gives every member a veto. The diversity is real: former French, British, Dutch, Spanish, Portuguese and American colonies, plus never-colonized Thailand; a junta, an absolute monarchy, one-party states and democracies; Muslim Indonesia, Catholic Philippines, Buddhist Thailand; and income gaps far wider than in the EU, with Singapore dozens of times richer per person than Cambodia. ASEAN claims centrality, leading the region's architecture, but has struggled with the 1997 financial crisis, the South China Sea and Myanmar's coup. Its institutions have little binding power and small budgets, so much cooperation happens in smaller groups, like the Malacca Straits Patrol. Still, its forums are useful meeting places: the Quad was born on the sidelines of ASEAN meetings.

Fourth, economic integration. The ASEAN Free Trade Area, launched in 1992, has eliminated nearly all internal tariffs, and ASEAN has trade deals with China, Japan, India, Australia and New Zealand, plus the RCEP mega-deal. But non-tariff barriers persist, only about a quarter of members' trade is with each other, the dispute settlement mechanism has never been used, and overlapping deals create a noodle bowl of rules. Millions migrate within the region, and remittances are vital for the Philippines, yet skilled workers struggle to move. Rich members like Singapore push liberalization, while poorer ones use consensus to protect sensitive sectors, like rice. After 1997, the Chiang Mai Initiative created currency swaps with China, Japan and South Korea.

Motion idea: a common ASEAN currency. For: no exchange rate risk, cheaper trade and remittances, and pressure for deeper integration. Against: most ASEAN trade is with outsiders, so stability against the dollar matters more; ASEAN is not an optimal currency area, lacking labor mobility, similar economies and fiscal transfers; and the poorest members would lose monetary control and suffer most.

Fifth, the South China Sea. China, Taiwan, the Philippines, Vietnam, Malaysia and Brunei have overlapping claims, and Indonesia contests Chinese claims near its waters. China's nine-dash line dates to the forties and fifties. It seized the Paracels from Vietnam in 1974, killed dozens of Vietnamese sailors near Johnson Reef in 1988 and took Mischief Reef in the nineties. In 2012 it took control of Scarborough Shoal, and ASEAN failed for the first time to issue a joint statement, blocked by Cambodia. In 2016, an international tribunal under the Law of the Sea ruled for the Philippines and rejected the nine-dash line; China ignored it. The stakes: over three trillion dollars of annual trade, oil, gas and fisheries. China's tactics: salami-slicing, small steps never worth a war; the cabbage strategy, surrounding features with layers of vessels; and building artificial islands topped with military bases. Malaysia and Brunei tend to play down disputes, the Philippines and Vietnam push back, and Cambodia and Laos block strong ASEAN statements.

Sixth, other challenges. Members like Indonesia want stronger human rights commitments, but authoritarian members invoke non-interference. ASEAN has helped mediate border disputes, like Thailand and Cambodia in 2008. Piracy thrives in the narrow Malacca Strait, fueled by underpaid, corruptible coast guards. And democracy is in retreat: Myanmar's coup, repeated coups in Thailand, Cambodia's dynastic rule and a growing military role in Indonesia's government. Military rule entrenches itself, kills, inspires copycat coups, leaves illiberal legacies and enriches officers at the public's expense.

Since the file was written: East Timor joined as ASEAN's eleventh member in 2025, and Malaysia, as chair, helped broker a ceasefire in the Thailand-Cambodia border war.
