Episode 28. Residential Zoning.
Two sides today: repeal zoning laws, and keep them.

First, repeal zoning.

Zoning creates housing crises. Bans on high-rises and new buildings restrict supply, permits and inspections raise costs, and approval uncertainty makes developers price higher. That is why rents in San Francisco and Manhattan run far above cities with fewer restrictions. More building lowers prices, frees money for local spending and limits gentrification and displacement.

Zoning hurts the economy. People who cannot afford the city get pushed into slums and sprawling suburbs, with long commutes, car dependence, destroyed ecosystems and expensive spread-out infrastructure. It blocks urbanization, which brings efficient public services, foreign investment, remittances and agglomeration benefits. It stops workers from moving to the best jobs. It makes housing bubbles worse, because rising demand cannot be met with new supply, so prices spike and crashes like 2008 hit harder. And fewer projects mean fewer construction jobs for low-skilled workers.

Zoning also enables discrimination. It has long been used to keep poor and minority families out of wealthy areas. Not In My Backyard politics, driven by affluent residents, turns zoning into a quiet tool of segregation that faces little scrutiny.

Now, keep zoning.

Framing: zoning mostly matters in cities. Housing stays cheap in rural and suburban areas either way.

Zoning helps development by slowing rapid urbanization, which drains rural areas of skilled people, money and political power, floods labor markets and drives down wages, and overloads services, causing overcrowding, poor sanitation and crime. It even prevents speculative bubbles: in a boom, developers borrow heavily to build, and when the bust comes, unfinished projects fail, banks take losses and bank runs follow. Zoning restrains that speculation.

Repeal could trigger a financial crisis. When prices fall, middle-class landlords lose their savings, rental income cannot cover mortgages, and seized homes are worth less, so banks, especially small ones, face losses, credit freezes and runs. Pension funds invested in real estate lose value, so people save more and spend less.

Planning is better in public hands. Governments have legal duties and answer to voters, while developers build for the rich. Houston, with almost no zoning, is still highly segregated. Zoning keeps strip clubs away from schools, and lets communities shape their own neighborhoods.

Zoning can even help marginalized people. Higher property values raise tax revenue for affordable housing, clear rules lower mortgage risk and therefore rents, and wealthy people unable to buy in exclusive areas move into poorer neighborhoods and spend money there. And if zoning is used to discriminate, the root cause is racism and classism, which will just find other tools, like restrictive deeds.
