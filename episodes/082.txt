Episode 82. Greek Debt Crisis.
Today: how Greece got into crisis, the three bailouts, and a puzzle: if Greece had real bargaining power, why did it accept such harsh terms?

First, the road to crisis. After its civil war, Greece grew rapidly with Marshall Plan help, and joined the European Economic Community in 1981. Then competition hurt Greek firms, and Papandreou's socialist government imposed price controls, nationalizations and wage indexation, leading to high inflation, weak growth and debt rising above a hundred percent of GDP. To join the euro, Greece tightened monetary policy but resisted fiscal reform, and creative accounting made its numbers look better than they were.

Second, euro euphoria. After joining in 2001, borrowing costs collapsed, markets assumed Greece would be bailed out, and Greek bonds yielded almost the same as German ones. Debt, inflation and the trade deficit soared, while uncompetitive industries and zombie state firms survived on cheap money.

Third, the crash. In 2009 the new government revealed the deficit was not three point seven percent but over fifteen. Bonds were downgraded to junk, spreads exploded, deposits fled, GDP fell by a quarter, unemployment hit about twenty-eight percent, a third of Greeks fell into poverty and hundreds of thousands emigrated.

Fourth, three bailouts from the Troika, the European Commission, European Central Bank and IMF. In 2010, a hundred and ten billion euros for deep cuts to deficits, pensions and health, and wage freezes. In 2012, a hundred and thirty billion, with a nearly thirty percent minimum wage cut, mass public layoffs and a fifty-three percent haircut for private bondholders. Politics exploded: mainstream parties collapsed, and far-left Syriza and far-right Golden Dawn surged. In 2015 Syriza's Alexis Tsipras played hardball, Greece defaulted to the IMF, and sixty-one percent voted no to austerity in a referendum. Yet days later Tsipras accepted a third, eighty-six billion euro bailout with the harshest terms yet.

Fifth, the bargaining puzzle. Greece looked weak: a small, peripheral economy with the most to lose from leaving the euro, no alternative lender, and unstable politics. But it had leverage: a default threatened the whole young eurozone and the recoveries of Portugal and Ireland; political chaos let negotiators claim their hands were tied; radical rhetoric helped in a game of chicken; the refugee crisis gave leverage; and the three Troika institutions disagreed with each other.

So why did Greece lose? Two reasons. Technocrats, not populists, led negotiations: economist Lucas Papademos as prime minister, and finance officials who privately believed reform was necessary, so politicians railed against austerity while negotiators gave ground. And ideology: Greece saw a macroeconomic problem, while creditors in Berlin and Brussels saw a moral failing, a lazy economy addicted to debt, and were willing to impose punishment at almost any cost.

That was Greek Debt Crisis. Next episode: Voter Turnout.
