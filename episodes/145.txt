Episode 145. Philippines.
Today: from Marcos to Marcos, Duterte's drug war, and the Philippines' turn back toward the U.S.

First, history. The Philippines was colonized by Spain and then the U.S., gaining independence in 1946, with the U.S. keeping economic privileges and military bases, and soon facing the communist Huk rebellion. Ferdinand Marcos, elected in 1965, built infrastructure but overspent to win re-election in 1969, forcing an IMF bailout that sparked inflation and protests. Citing bombings and alleged communist plots, he declared martial law in 1972 and ruled as a dictator: torture and killings were widespread, and despite growth, debt, corruption and poverty soared. In 1983, opposition leader Ninoy Aquino was assassinated on returning from exile. After Marcos stole the 1986 election from his widow, Corazon Aquino, the peaceful People Power revolution, with around two million protesters, forced Marcos to flee. Democracy has survived since, but with corruption scandals, a president forced out over graft, Muslim insurgencies in the south and poverty.

Second, Duterte. Elected in 2016, the populist former mayor of Davao launched a drug war that killed thousands, mostly extrajudicially. In 2017 the army retook the city of Marawi from Islamic State-linked fighters. He also tilted toward China and withdrew from the International Criminal Court when it examined his drug war.

Third, Bongbong Marcos. The dictator's son won the 2022 election in a landslide, with a campaign that whitewashed his father's abuses. His risks include weak legislative wins, like the stalled Maharlika sovereign fund, inflation and Chinese economic retaliation.

Fourth, the economy. A middle-income country driven by consumer spending, it exports electronics and outsourcing services and depends heavily on remittances from workers abroad. China is its largest trading partner.

Fifth, security. A 2014 defense agreement let U.S. forces return to Philippine bases, and in 2023 Marcos expanded it to nine sites. Clashes with Chinese vessels in the South China Sea, including collisions and water cannon attacks, have pushed Manila closer to the U.S., which calls its commitment ironclad, and to Japan and Australia. Insurgency in Mindanao remains a domestic threat.

Since the file was written: Marcos and the Duterte family fell out bitterly. Rodrigo Duterte was arrested in 2025 and sent to the ICC in The Hague, his daughter, Vice President Sara Duterte, was impeached, and a huge scandal over flood control funds sparked mass protests.
