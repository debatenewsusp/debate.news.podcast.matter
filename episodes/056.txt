Episode 56. IMF.
Today: how the IMF works, how it lends, debt restructuring, austerity, and the main criticisms.

First, the basics. The IMF has around a hundred and ninety members and close to a trillion dollars in resources. The U.S. is the largest contributor with about seventeen percent of votes, enough to veto decisions that need an eighty-five percent supermajority. The IMF lends to countries in balance of payments crises, monitors economies and gives technical advice. During COVID it ramped up emergency lending and issued six hundred and fifty billion dollars in Special Drawing Rights. China is underrepresented relative to its economy, and is building alternatives like the Asian Infrastructure Investment Bank and the BRICS New Development Bank.

Second, lending. There are short-term facilities, longer programs for chronic problems, rapid funding for shocks like disasters, and newer loans for climate and pandemic resilience, all capped relative to a country's quota. The process: a country applies, the IMF assesses debt sustainability, negotiates conditions like spending cuts or floating the currency, the board approves, and money is released in stages as targets are met.

Third, debt restructuring. The IMF faces a paradox: restoring debt sustainability by lending more to over-indebted countries. So it often requires private creditors to take losses, like Greece's sixty-four percent haircut in 2012. That is hard: without collective action clauses, holdouts can block deals; restructuring domestic debt hurts local banks; unbalanced deals look like theft to creditors; and the IMF itself, as a preferred creditor, never takes losses.

Fourth, austerity. Conditions historically followed the Washington Consensus: cut deficits and subsidies, broaden taxes, float the currency, liberalize trade and investment, and privatize. More recently: wage freezes, value-added taxes and spending cuts.

Fifth, criticisms. One, market fundamentalism: staff are trained in free-market economics, so the IMF blames governments rather than markets and prioritizes budgets and inflation over jobs. Two, poor information: technocrats in Washington rely on spreadsheets, talk mainly to finance ministers in luxury hotels for a few weeks, and miss local political realities. Three, unequal power: the U.S. holds a veto, borrowers negotiate with their backs against the wall, and the IMF keeps expanding its mandate. Four, political instability: the IMF is seen as neocolonial, since its chief is always European, conditions override sovereignty, tax hikes and subsidy cuts hit the poor, rich countries keep their own farm subsidies, and deals are made in secret. Five, programs fail by design: one-size-fits-all rules, rushed timetables and contradictory conditions.

Finally, recent programs include Argentina, Pakistan, Sri Lanka, Egypt, Ghana and Zambia, and since COVID the IMF has expanded access for poor countries and softened some conditions.
