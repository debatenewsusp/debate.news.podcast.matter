Episode 83. Voter Turnout.
Today: why turnout matters, who votes, why anyone votes at all, and how election forecasts affect it.

First, why it matters. Low turnout skews results toward groups that vote more, like older and wealthier citizens, and a government elected by a small share of voters struggles to claim legitimacy.

Second, who votes. More educated, wealthier and older people vote more, and turnout rises when races look competitive. Disadvantaged groups vote less, often facing restrictive laws and higher costs to voting.

Third, why vote at all? Voting is a collective action problem: everyone benefits from democracy, but one vote almost never decides an election. Several theories try to explain it. Anthony Downs's rational choice view: people vote when the benefit of their candidate winning outweighs the cost of voting. Riker and Ordeshook add civic duty: the tiny chance of being decisive, multiplied by how much you care, plus the satisfaction of doing your democratic duty. Ferejohn and Fiorina's minimax regret: under uncertainty, people vote to avoid the worst feeling, their candidate losing by one vote. Game theory: voters coordinate as groups, which raises the payoff of turning out. And John Aldrich notes voting is now cheap and easy, with easy registration, nearby polling stations, abundant media and strong democratic norms, so small benefits are enough.

Finally, forecasting. If people vote more when they think their vote matters, horse-race coverage can suppress turnout. Forecasts make outcomes look more certain than they are, because audiences ignore margins of error, and pollsters often underestimate their own errors.
