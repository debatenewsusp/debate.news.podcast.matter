Episode 90. Lesbian Separatism.
Today: Simone de Beauvoir's critique of love and marriage in The Second Sex, and why she saw advantages in relationships between women.

First, Beauvoir's ideal. Authentic love requires two free people who recognize each other's freedom: neither gives up their own projects, and both discover values in the world together. This flows from existentialism's core idea that existence precedes essence: we are thrown into circumstances we did not choose, but we create meaning by freely choosing projects that take us beyond them. Poverty, ignorance and oppression limit that freedom, and Beauvoir focused on how gender roles limit women's.

Second, women's oppression. Historically, women were confined to passive roles, marriage and motherhood, treated as property passed from father to husband, existing for others rather than for themselves. Dependence can feel comfortable, since it relieves you of responsibility, like a woman who prefers a golden cage to poverty and loneliness. But it is bad faith: escaping the task of justifying your own life, and it easily becomes possessive and tyrannical.

Third, marriage. Beauvoir argued marriage kills love: it drains erotic magic, buries love under habit, and daily intimacy breeds neither understanding nor sympathy. Marriage was never designed for love; it imposes traditional roles, pushes women to put husbands' careers first, and functioned historically as legally endorsed servitude, with sexual and domestic service traded for protection.

Fourth, love is asymmetric. Men, even when devoted, remain sovereign subjects for whom a woman is one value among many. Women are pushed to treat love as salvation by proxy: worshipping the lover, adopting his friends and opinions, dissolving the I into a we. This fails because making another person your life project means disowning yourself; because passion fades, after which women try to bind men through children or marriage; and because treating a relationship as a project turns the other person into a means rather than an end.

Finally, relationships between women. Beauvoir saw attraction to the female body as an understandable response to women's history of male domination. And she argued these relationships had real advantages: less shaped by the conventions of marriage, so more sincere; less to hide between lovers who are alike, so more authentic; and less distorted by power struggles, allowing a more equal, collaborative and reciprocal love.
