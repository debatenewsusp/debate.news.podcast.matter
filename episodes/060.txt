Episode 60. Strategic Decoupling.
Today: America's strategy of de-risking from China, who benefits, critical minerals, the Inflation Reduction Act, and U.S.-EU trade. This reflects the file's 2023 snapshot.

First, Biden's strategy had three parts. Diversify supply chains through subsidies, tariffs, coordination with allies and pushes for friend-shoring. Penalize China with tariffs, sanctions like putting Huawei on the U.S. Entity List, and export controls on advanced chips, even persuading the Netherlands to restrict ASML's chipmaking machines. And build industry at home through the Inflation Reduction Act, the CHIPS Act and the infrastructure law. China hit back with export limits on gallium and germanium, mostly symbolic since other countries can produce them, but a warning given China's dominance in materials like polysilicon and aluminum.

Second, the winners. Mexico, through nearshoring: exports, car production and foreign investment surged, with firms like Ternium and Unilever building plants. Firms move there to avoid tariffs on China, cut shipping costs, and benefit from stronger IP protection, the USMCA trade deal and a stable peso. Southeast Asia and India: after Trump's tariffs and China's zero-COVID lockdowns, Apple and Foxconn expanded into India and Vietnam, Samsung shut its Chinese production and became Vietnam's largest foreign investor, and Lego is building a billion-dollar factory in Vietnam.

Third, critical minerals. Chile holds a huge share of the world's lithium, and President Boric moved to bring it under state partnership. Peru and Chile lead in copper. Indonesia, the biggest nickel producer, banned raw nickel exports to force companies, mostly Chinese, to build processing plants at home. Without a U.S. trade deal, American firms get fewer subsidies there. Allies coordinate through the Minerals Security Partnership, and the EU's Critical Raw Materials Act limits dependence on any single supplier.

Fourth, the Inflation Reduction Act: hundreds of billions in tax credits for renewable energy, home efficiency, electric cars and domestic manufacturing of batteries, solar and wind components, plus green lending. It sparked hundreds of projects, especially in a new battery belt across the Midwest and South. Critics point to slow environmental permitting, which delays clean projects more than established fossil fuels. Europe complained about the buy-American rules as firms moved production to the U.S., and responded with its own Green Deal Industrial Plan and looser rules on subsidies.

Finally, U.S.-EU trade. There is no free trade agreement despite over a trillion dollars in annual trade. Trump-era fights, over digital taxes, steel tariffs and Boeing versus Airbus, were settled under Biden, and both sides now coordinate on Russia sanctions. Flashpoints remain: tariffs on seafood and cars, EU media rules, data privacy, agricultural protection like protected food names, U.S. blocking of WTO appointments, and different philosophies of regulation.

That was Strategic Decoupling. Next episode: Children's Media.
