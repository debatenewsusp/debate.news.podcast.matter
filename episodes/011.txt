Episode 11. Military Invasions.
Two sides today: defending military invasions, and opposing them.

First, defending invasions.

Sometimes nothing else works. Sanctions get evaded, and regimes that cannot afford to look weak refuse diplomacy. For major, protracted crises, invasion may be the only real option.

Invasions are also likely to succeed. Leaders who approve them face intense scrutiny, so failure is political suicide. Western technology and expertise can overpower opposing forces, and shock-and-awe tactics demoralize troops and push commanders to defect, especially with promises of amnesty. Neighboring states also want a quick end to avoid refugee crises.

And the harms can be limited. Precision weapons reduce collateral damage, and heavy media scrutiny deters abuses by soldiers.

Now, opposing invasions.

There are alternatives: targeted sanctions, humanitarian aid, and organic revolutionary pressure that does not need foreign troops.

Invasions often fail. Foreign intervention, especially Western intervention, rallies people behind abusive regimes. Defenders know the terrain. Public support at home is thin, so when body bags come home, there is pressure to withdraw fast, leaving a power vacuum. Civilians may resist, making it hard to tell fighters from non-combatants. Dense forests or mountains make airpower less useful and force bloody infantry warfare. And even after a quick victory, loyalists can wage long guerrilla insurgencies.

Collateral damage is enormous, and abusive regimes use civilians as human shields. Occupation is the hardest part: power vacuums get filled by radicals, and infighting tends to elevate the most extreme leader.

Key examples. In Afghanistan, the 2001 invasion toppled the Taliban, but a twenty-year insurgency followed, and the Taliban retook Kabul after the U.S. withdrew. In Iraq, the 2003 invasion was justified by false claims about weapons of mass destruction. The occupation was badly mismanaged, with billions in aid unaccounted for, sectarian violence exploded, and the instability later helped the Islamic State rise. On the success side, in Gambia in 2017 West African troops forced a president who lost an election to step down within two days. And in Haiti in 1994, U.S. troops restored the elected president with minimal casualties after sanctions had failed.
