Episode 97. U.S. versus Google.
Today: the U.S. government's antitrust case against Google search.

First, the charges. The Justice Department argued Google is a monopoly with around ninety percent of global search, and that it blocked rivals by paying Apple, Samsung and Mozilla to be the default search engine and by preloading its services on Android phones.

Second, Google's defense. Paying to be the default is legal, like cereal brands paying supermarkets for eye-level shelves. It faces real competition, since people search on Amazon and TikTok. And it may simply be the best product.

Third, what the government had to prove: that Judge Amit Mehta should define the search market narrowly, so Amazon and TikTok do not count, and that the default deals actually shut rivals out. It was the first major tech antitrust trial since the Microsoft case of the nineties.

Since then: in 2024 Judge Mehta ruled that Google illegally maintained a search monopoly. In the remedies phase in 2025, he declined to force a sale of the Chrome browser but restricted exclusive default deals and required some data sharing with rivals.

That was U.S. versus Google. Next episode: Sam Bankman-Fried.
