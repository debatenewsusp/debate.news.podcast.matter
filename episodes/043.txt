Episode 43. Agriculture.
Today: basic facts, how productivity grows, and how the agricultural value chain works.

First, the facts. Agriculture is only around four percent of global GDP, but it is vital for food security, trade and raw materials like ethanol and cotton. About three-quarters of the world's poor live in rural areas, and in many low-income countries most people work in farming, over eighty percent in Burundi. Around five hundred million households rely on small family farms, and as many on herding. Top products are sugar cane, maize, rice, wheat, milk and potatoes. Around three-quarters of farmland is used for livestock. As countries develop, farming's share shrinks: in the U.S. it fell from sixty percent of the economy in 1860 to fifteen percent fifty years later.

Second, productivity. Since the sixties, farm output has grown around two percent a year while land use barely grew, thanks to science and technology: machinery, fertilizers, irrigation and genetic engineering. Fertilizers add nutrients and raise yields. Drip irrigation can cut water use by eighty percent. Genetically modified crops resist pests and drought. Automation, like self-driving tractors and soil sensors, cuts labor costs. Good practices matter too, like crop rotation, cover crops, integrated pest management and mixed planting, while slash-and-burn farming is destructive.

Third, the value chain. Input suppliers sell seeds, fertilizers and pesticides made by giants like Bayer, which owns Monsanto, Syngenta, BASF and Corteva. Then come growers: smallholders farming for subsistence and selling surplus, workers on large estates, industrial agribusinesses, and cooperatives like Israel's kibbutzim. Finally, processing turns raw crops into food, for example wheat into flour and then bread, led by firms like Cargill and ADM.
