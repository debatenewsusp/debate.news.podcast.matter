Episode 75. Judicial Term Limits.
Today: the case for term limits on top judges, and the case against.

First, term limits make appointments regular. Without them, deaths and retirements decide how many seats each president fills, justices retire strategically when their party is in power, and parties pick young nominees to extend their influence. Some parties gain outsized power by pure luck. Since politics will shape courts anyway, it is fairer for every party to get the same number of picks.

Second, they balance independence and democracy. Individual judges stay insulated from politics, but the court's overall makeup tracks elected officials through regular nominations.

Third, they cool the judicial arms race. Parties politicize courts when the benefits outweigh the costs, and once one side does, the other follows. Term limits reduce the payoff, since each justice serves fewer years. They raise the cost, by creating a norm against politicization. They reduce the incentive to nominate extremists, who are chosen because moderates sometimes defect, as Anthony Kennedy often did. And they lower the stakes of confirmation fights, since a seat is no longer a lifetime prize.

Fourth, they make courts work better. Judges leave before cognitive decline, which impeachment cannot address, so judges rather than clerks hold real power. Nominees are chosen on merit rather than youth. Turnover brings fresh perspectives closer to public opinion and keeps judges in touch with ordinary life. And former justices can mentor the lower courts.

Now, the case against. Turnover creates doctrinal instability. Life tenure protects independence, while term-limited judges may rule with their next job in mind. Litigants may time cases around expected appointments. Judges racing the clock might rush rulings. And guaranteeing every president several picks could make nominations an even bigger election issue, with more frequent bruising confirmation hearings.
