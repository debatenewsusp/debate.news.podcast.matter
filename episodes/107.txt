Episode 107. China's Irredentism.
Three territories today: Hong Kong, Tibet and Taiwan.

First, Hong Kong. Britain handed Hong Kong back to China in 1997 as a special administrative region under Deng Xiaoping's One Country, Two Systems: part of China, but with its own legal, political and financial systems, guaranteed for fifty years until 2047, with the Basic Law as its mini-constitution. Tensions grew over Beijing's influence, from a 2003 push for patriotic education to the 2015 disappearance of booksellers critical of Beijing who turned up in mainland custody. In 2019, after a Hong Kong man killed his girlfriend in Taiwan, Chief Executive Carrie Lam backed a bill allowing extradition to mainland China. Hong Kongers feared it would let Beijing silence critics, and by June hundreds of thousands, by some counts far more, marched. Lam withdrew the bill in September, but it was too little, too late: protesters now demanded universal suffrage, and clashes turned violent, with a protester shot and a student, Chow Tsz-lok, dying in November. In 2020 Beijing imposed a sweeping National Security Law that lets authorities treat activists as terrorists, effectively ending the protest movement and much of Hong Kong's autonomy.

Second, Tibet, a mostly Tibetan Buddhist region. The new People's Republic invaded in 1950, and in 1951 the Dalai Lama was pressured into the Seventeen Point Agreement, recognizing Chinese rule with promised autonomy, which he later repudiated. After a failed uprising in 1959, he fled to India and set up a government in exile. The Great Leap Forward caused great suffering, the Cultural Revolution destroyed thousands of monasteries, and Tibet remains officially autonomous but tightly controlled, with accusations of cultural genocide and a heavy military crackdown after protests in 2008.

Third, Taiwan. After losing the civil war in 1949, the Nationalist KMT fled to Taiwan and ruled under martial law during the White Terror, while the West recognized it as China's government. Export-led growth and U.S. aid created the Taiwan Miracle, one of the Asian Tigers. In 1971 the UN gave China's seat to the People's Republic, and Nixon visited Beijing soon after. Under Chiang Ching-kuo, Taiwan gradually democratized: the 1979 Kaohsiung Incident, a crackdown on pro-democracy protesters, became a turning point, martial law ended in 1987, and the KMT lost the presidency in 2000. Beijing grew alarmed at pro-independence leaders, passing an Anti-Secession Law in 2005 that authorizes force, and tensions rose further after Tsai Ing-wen of the Democratic Progressive Party won in 2016 and took a call from President-elect Trump. China now pressures countries and companies that recognize Taiwan and regularly sends military aircraft near the island. In 2024, the DPP's Lai Ching-te won the presidency, keeping the party in power.

That was China's Irredentism. Next episode: China's Military Modernization.
