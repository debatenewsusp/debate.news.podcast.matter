Episode 64. Sexual Violence.
Today: how common it is, why it goes unreported, its causes, why prosecution is hard, recent improvements, and a framing debate.

First, frequency. The WHO estimates about one in three women experience sexual or physical violence in their lifetime, with higher rates in poorer countries. Indigenous, Black, trans and disabled women face higher risk.

Second, underreporting. Most assaults are never reported. Victims fear not being believed, being blamed and questioned about clothing, drinking or past partners, and retaliation, especially from a boss or relative. Trauma causes memory gaps and self-blame, and many do not even label what happened as rape. Low conviction rates make the ordeal of a trial seem pointless.

Third, causes. Social learning theory says children who experience or witness abuse may repeat it. Patriarchal norms promote male dominance, objectify women and blame victims. Radical feminists argue rape functions as a tool of patriarchy that makes women police their own lives, with good men cast as protectors from bad men. Media and pornography can normalize aggression and trivialize consent. Insecurity, a desire for power, heavy drinking and certain peer cultures add risk, and racial history, including the rape of enslaved women, still shapes patterns today.

Fourth, why prosecution is hard. Prosecutors avoid shaky cases, and late reports may hit statutes of limitations. There are usually no witnesses, so juries weigh competing stories and often doubt victims. Prosecutors must prove intent, and defendants exploit stereotypes, claiming they believed there was consent. Trauma can make testimony seem inconsistent. Forensic exams are often unavailable, costly or invasive, and police may lack resources or will. Victims may be pressured into silence, feel shame, distrust the system or simply want to move on.

Fifth, improvements. Rape shield laws stop questioning about a victim's sexual history, many places dropped outdated rules requiring prompt reporting or corroboration, and NGOs run crisis centers and hotlines.

Finally, a framing debate: is rape about violence or sex? Campaigns stress violence to reject the idea of a crime of passion and highlight power and anger. Critics say this ignores its sexual nature, can invalidate sexual trauma, and wrongly implies that rape requires physical force.
