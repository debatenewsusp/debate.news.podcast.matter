Episode 23. Drug Legalization.
Today: the case for legalizing all drugs, and the case against.

First, the model. Consumption and possession become legal. Production is tightly regulated like medicine: tested for purity, sold only by licensed sellers like pharmacies, with clean needles and clear labels on risks and safe doses.

Argument one: it is a principled duty. People have a right to bodily autonomy, which includes what they put into their bodies, even if it is self-destructive, just as we allow dangerous jobs. The state should only intervene when harm to others is direct and certain. Drinking is legal while drunk driving is not; drug use and crimes committed on drugs should be treated the same way. Drug laws are also applied in deeply racist ways. Addiction is an illness that removes control, so punishing it punishes something beyond people's control. And many people use drugs to cope with poverty and despair the state failed to fix, so prosecuting them punishes them twice.

Argument two: harm reduction. Prohibition does not kill demand, it pushes it into a black market run by cartels, with impure drugs laced with fentanyl, dirty needles and violence. A legal, regulated market is safer, more trusted and cheaper, since pharmaceutical firms do not have to smuggle, so the black market withers. Regulation means pure drugs and safe doses, users seek help without fear of arrest, stigma falls, and tax revenue plus savings from the drug war can fund rehabilitation. Will use rise? Probably not much, since most people who want drugs already use them. New users would mostly try lower-risk drugs, and use may even fall as the forbidden-fruit appeal disappears.

Argument three: it protects third parties. In the U.S. the prison population grew over five hundred percent since the seventies, and Black men are far more likely to be jailed for possession, from sentencing gaps like crack versus cocaine to weak public defenders and coercive plea deals. Prison makes crime worse: ex-inmates face stigma, unemployment and prison gangs, and their families suffer. Legalization also starves cartels of revenue and removes the easy money gangs use to recruit young people.

Now, the case against.

States legitimately protect people from their own irrational choices, like seatbelt laws, and drugs harm your future self through addiction. Someone in the grip of hard drugs cannot meaningfully consent, and there are third-party harms. In practice, legalization normalizes drugs, increases access, lets companies advertise and lobby, and makes drugs look safe. Users build tolerance, escalate to harder drugs and return to the black market for what pharmacies will not sell or to avoid taxes. And it creates a drug-industrial complex: think Purdue Pharma and the opioid crisis.
