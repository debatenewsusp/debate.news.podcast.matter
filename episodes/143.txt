Episode 143. Laos.
Today: a one-party state deep in debt and close to China.

First, politics. Laos became independent from France in 1954 and fell into civil war between the communist Pathet Lao, backed by North Vietnam, and the royal government, backed by the CIA. The U.S. bombed Laos so heavily that it is the most bombed country per person in history, and unexploded bombs still kill people today. The communists won in 1975 and set up a single-party state under the Lao People's Revolutionary Party. It moved from a Soviet-style economy to a mixed market economy in the eighties. Dissent is banned, the media is state-controlled and crackdowns intensified in 2023.

Second, the economy. Laos faces a debt crisis: years of borrowing from China, including for a high-speed railway, pushed public debt above a hundred and twenty percent of GDP, and the weak kip makes repayment harder. China is likely to quietly defer payments. Laos earns money by exporting hydropower, mining and forestry, and its cheap labor could attract factories leaving China. The government is cutting spending, reforming state firms and running a top-down anti-corruption campaign.

Third, foreign policy. Laos is landlocked, has no territorial disputes with China, and China is its largest investor and creditor, so it is a reliably pro-China voice. It is also close to fellow communist Vietnam and to Cambodia. Thailand is its largest trading partner, buying its electricity and employing Lao migrant workers who send money home. Laos has stayed silent on Ukraine and holds joint military exercises with Russia, straining ties with the West.
