Episode 62. Pharmaceuticals.
Today: how drugs are developed, how regulators approve them, the industry's market power, the revolving door, and medicalization.

First, development. Scientists identify a target molecule linked to a disease, screen huge libraries of compounds for hits, and refine the best into leads. Preclinical tests in cells and animals check toxicity, how the drug acts on the body and how the body processes it, and set starting doses. Then come human trials: phase one tests safety in a few dozen people, phase two tests effectiveness in hundreds, and phase three compares it to standard treatment in thousands, often double-blind. After approval, phase four monitors long-term effects. The whole process takes ten to fifteen years and over two billion dollars, and the vast majority of candidates fail.

Second, regulation. The FDA in the U.S. and the EMA in Europe review all the data. The FDA gained the power to require proof of effectiveness in 1962, after the thalidomide scandal. Critics talk of death by regulation: regulators are punished for approving a bad drug but rarely for delaying a good one, so they become excessively cautious, and some useful drugs were delayed by years. Scandals pile on layers of red tape, outdated protocols persist, and compliance is slow and costly.

Third, the industry tends toward monopoly: costs are huge, success is rare, and patents protect single products, while demand for medicine is inelastic, which allows price gouging. Firms extend monopolies through tricks: patent hopping to a nearly identical new version, minor reformulations like once-daily pills, patenting new uses, and patenting one mirror-image form of an existing molecule. They also pay generic makers to delay entry, file petitions to slow rivals and lobby heavily.

Fourth, the revolving door. Regulators who came from industry are sympathetic to it, those hoping to join industry later go easy on it, and departing officials carry insider knowledge and contacts. Agencies get flooded with industry-slanted information and absorb industry thinking through constant contact. Examples include former FDA commissioner Scott Gottlieb joining Pfizer's board soon after leaving office.

Fifth, medicalization: more and more of life is defined as a medical problem needing drugs. Critics say it labels deviance or normal aging as illness, as with treating ordinary aging or mild sadness with pills, feeds anxiety through advertising and disease mongering, raises costs, replaces proven therapy with medication, and expands state medical power. Defenders say it brings real care to previously ignored conditions like depression, reduces stigma by showing some conditions are beyond people's control, improves medical training and builds trust between doctors and patients.
