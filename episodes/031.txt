Episode 31. Terrorism.
Today: the main groups, how terrorism is financed, why stopping the money is hard, and where the weapons come from.

First, the groups. Active ones include ISIS, al-Qaeda, al-Shabaab in Somalia, Boko Haram in Nigeria, Hezbollah in Lebanon, Hamas in Gaza, the Pakistani Taliban, the Haqqani Network and the PKK. Past examples include the IRA, Spain's ETA, Sri Lanka's Tamil Tigers, Colombia's FARC and Peru's Shining Path.

Second, financing. Groups need money for weapons, fighters, bribes and propaganda; ISIS spent over a billion dollars a year at its peak. They raise it through sympathetic donors and diasporas, crime like kidnapping, extortion and drug trafficking, selling commodities like oil and diamonds, and state sponsors. ISIS earned around a million dollars a day from oil, Iran funds Hezbollah with hundreds of millions a year, and FARC ran on narcotics.

Third, why cutting off finance is hard. Banks and finance ministries lack counter-terrorism expertise. Groups launder through shell companies, informal hawala networks and crypto. Transactions are done by low-level middlemen, not famous leaders, and intelligence is rarely shared with banks. Much is settled in cash in places cut off from the banking system, and crackdowns end up hurting refugees and families that rely on remittances. One state's terrorist is another's freedom fighter, so enforcement is patchy. Legitimate and illegitimate business blur, as with IRA-run taxis and hotels. And terrorism is cheap: bombs need fertilizer and batteries, and rifles are mass-produced and easy to buy.

Fourth, weapons. Cold War proxy wars flooded Latin America, Africa, the Middle East and Southeast Asia with arms, fueling black markets like the tri-border area of Argentina, Brazil and Paraguay, or the Sahel, where an AK-47 costs a few hundred dollars. Groups also seize weapons, like militias raiding Libyan depots after Gaddafi fell or Boko Haram attacking Nigerian armories, buy them from corrupt officers, or capture them on the battlefield.
