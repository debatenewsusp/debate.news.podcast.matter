Episode 106. China's SOEs.
Today: the problems with China's state-owned enterprises, attempts at reform, why reform stalls, and local government debt.

First, the problems. State firms are heavily indebted and less efficient than private ones. Private companies produce around sixty percent of GDP, seventy percent of innovation, eighty percent of urban jobs and ninety percent of new jobs, with champions like Huawei. State firms enjoy cheap loans and lobby for rules that squeeze out private rivals, and many would not survive without those perks. Xi's anti-corruption purges also removed executives, leaving firms without coherent strategy.

Second, reform. The state asset regulator, SASAC, created in 2003, followed a policy of grasping the big and releasing the small: privatizing or merging smaller firms and turning the remaining giants into corporations with their own boards. China Unicom sold around eleven billion dollars in shares to private investors to make it more profit-focused while keeping state control. Beijing still wants state firms to be bigger and stronger in strategic sectors like defense, energy, telecoms, aviation and railways. Supporters argue state-guided coordination built cities like Shenzhen and Hangzhou, alongside tax cuts for entrepreneurs and state venture funds for deep tech.

Third, why reform fails. Top executives hold ministerial rank, sometimes equal to the agencies supervising them, and have powerful personal networks and family ties to leaders. Many are late-career appointees with no hope of promotion, so they have little reason to disrupt a status quo that benefits them.

Fourth, local government financing vehicles. Local governments cannot easily borrow directly, so they create state companies that borrow to build infrastructure. The risks: the collateral backing this debt is opaque and may be worth far less than the loans; cash-strapped local governments are increasingly unwilling to bail them out; and banks have grown wary of lending to them. Local governments are also borrowing new money just to repay old debts, crowding out new investment, which adds to fears for China's huge bond market, already shaken by property developers like Evergrande.
