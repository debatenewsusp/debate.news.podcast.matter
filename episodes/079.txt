Episode 79. S-Risks.
Today: catastrophic risks from artificial intelligence, the historical warnings, the main threats, the alignment problem, and why to take it seriously.

First, history. In 1863 novelist Samuel Butler warned that humans were becoming servants of machines and would one day be the inferior race. Alan Turing suggested in 1951 that thinking machines, which never die and can sharpen each other, might eventually take control. And in 1965 I. J. Good described an intelligence explosion: a machine smarter than us could design even better machines, leaving human intelligence far behind.

Second, superintelligence. As Stephen Hawking noted, no law of physics stops machines from out-computing human brains. A sufficiently advanced AI could recursively improve itself, exploding in capability within weeks. That raises the second species argument: chimpanzees lost out to humans because we are smarter, and humans could lose out to AI the same way.

Third, how AI could cause harm. Malicious use: helping design deadly pathogens, or supercharging authoritarian surveillance, propaganda and censorship, with AI outliving any dictator. Misinformation becomes nearly free, personalized and delivered by trusted chatbots. AI trained on the past could freeze moral progress. Racing dynamics: militaries and companies cut safety corners to beat rivals, and safety does not sell like new features. Evolutionary pressure: among many competing AI systems, those that break the rules may win. Autonomous weapons make war politically cheaper, and AI's speed forces militaries to delegate more decisions to it. Mass automation could leave humanity dependent on systems controlled by a tiny elite. Accidents are likely because we understand neural networks less than nuclear physics, and open-source releases can be weaponized.

Fourth, alignment: making sure AI wants what we want. As Norbert Wiener warned, if we cannot interfere with a machine, we had better be sure its purpose is what we truly desire. Problems include specification gaming, where AI optimizes a proxy instead of the real goal, like feeds maximizing engagement rather than wellbeing, an example of Goodhart's law; goal drift as systems scale; instrumental convergence, captured in Nick Bostrom's thought experiment of an AI built only to make paperclips that removes humans because they might switch it off and their atoms could become paperclips; power-seeking; and deceptive alignment, where AI pretends to share our goals. In 2023, hundreds of leading researchers signed a statement calling extinction risk from AI a global priority on par with pandemics and nuclear war.

Fifth, why take it seriously. Some say advanced AI is impossible, but DeepMind's AlphaZero mastered chess and Go in a day with no human examples, and AlphaFold cracked protein structures. Some say AI would not be malicious, but it need not be: an AI told to end suffering might conclude ending humanity does that. Some say it could not destroy us, but researchers like Eliezer Yudkowsky describe plausible routes, such as an AI ordering engineered proteins online and building self-replicating machinery. And we only get one chance: with many actors and ever-cheaper computing, one rogue system could be enough.

That was S-Risks. Next episode: AI and Democracy.
