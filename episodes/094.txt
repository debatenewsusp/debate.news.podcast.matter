Episode 94. International Political Economy.
Today: the big theories of how politics and economics interact globally, the history of hegemony, and why trade deals stall.

First, the shift. After the Cold War, economic liberalism, open and integrated markets, was the consensus. China's state-led success, the 2008 crash and the globalization backlash broke it. Trump and Biden both embraced protectionism, with tariffs, Buy American rules, the CHIPS Act and the IRA. The elephant curve shows why: globalization's big winners were Western elites and China's middle class, while Western middle-class wages stagnated. History swings between states and markets: mercantilism, then British-led liberalism, then interwar state control, then postwar American-led markets, and now rising economic nationalism.

Second, three theories. Economic nationalism: states dominate markets to maximize power and security, since wealth funds military strength in an anarchic world. Old mercantilism hoarded trade surpluses; today's version fears dependence, like the U.S. on Chinese rare earths, and uses infant industry protection, Buy National policies and plans like Made in China 2025, echoing Alexander Hamilton's call for a strong state role in industry. The catch: it makes cooperation harder and economic change can spark conflict.

Marxism: capitalism is coercive and exploitative, the capitalist class is a transnational community, think Davos, and the state is its tool. For Lenin, imperialism was capitalism's final stage: falling profits at home drive capitalists to seize colonies, and uneven growth causes wars. Critics note that Keynesian policy and multinationals with anti-war interests weakened this story, but the 2008 crisis revived Marxist arguments that the system breeds crises and inequality.

Hegemonic stability theory: an open world economy needs one dominant power. Left alone, states open where they are strong and protect where they are weak, a prisoner's dilemma. A hegemon benefits most from openness and acts as system manager: keeping its market open in crises, providing a currency like the dollar, giving aid and managing crises. Hence mercantilism without a hegemon, liberalism under Pax Britannica, collapse between the wars, and liberalism under the U.S. after 1945.

Third, Britain's rise and fall. After Napoleon, Britain dominated, but only embraced free trade after repealing the Corn Laws, then spread it through gunboat diplomacy, access to its market and treaties, under the gold standard. Then a rising, nationalist Germany challenged it, much like China today, while technology spread, rivals copied cheaply, and Britain overconsumed and overspent on war. Unlike Britain, the U.S. has allies, a tech edge, immigration and the dollar.

Fourth, the Depression. Economist Charles Kindleberger argued Britain was willing to lead but unable, and the U.S. able but unwilling. Disastrous choices followed: war reparations reversed capital flows, the 1930 Smoot-Hawley tariff triggered cascading protectionism, Britain rejoined gold at an overvalued rate, countries competitively devalued, and the world split into blocs. Lessons: integrate defeated powers, keep markets open, keep capital flowing and spend countercyclically.

Fifth, U.S. trade policy. Congress controls trade under the Constitution, but since 1934 it has delegated negotiating power to presidents, and fast-track authority lets Congress vote deals up or down. After 1945, the GATT cut tariffs from around forty percent to under ten, though agriculture and textiles were kept out. Today the problem is non-tariff barriers, like safety, environmental and cultural rules, which are much harder to negotiate. The Uruguay Round created the WTO in 1995 but left buyer's remorse in both the global South and the West, sparking the 1999 Seattle protests, and the Doha Round failed. Obama's Trans-Pacific Partnership aimed to contain China by targeting state subsidies and tech transfers, but unions, environmentalists and both parties turned against it, and Trump abandoned it. Biden's Indo-Pacific framework offers no new market access, so it has attracted little support.
