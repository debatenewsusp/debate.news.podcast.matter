Episode 21. Money versus Happiness.
Today: defending money, opposing money, and a classic motion on earning to give.

First, defending money.

Money may have diminishing returns, but poverty has escalating costs. Poverty means sleepless nights over bills and food, and it strains relationships.

Money does buy happiness. It pays for travel, good food and nice things, and you do not get bored because wealth lets you keep changing experiences. It also follows your passions as they change through life, from travel to art school to golf. It is a buffer against recessions, disasters, wars and automation. It buys time, through earlier retirement, less overtime and better healthcare. And it lets you care for people you love, like paying off your parents' mortgage.

Now, opposing money.

Returns diminish once you are comfortable, and luxury stops feeling special once it is routine. Wealth makes you compare yourself with even richer people, doubt whether people love you or your money, and sometimes feel guilty. Most people find life worth living without ever getting rich, which shows that relationships, not possessions, give life meaning. And chasing money has costs: expensive cities, ethically shady careers, cut-throat competition and a culture of greed.

Finally, the motion: this house would take a lucrative career and donate much of the income, rather than work in the nonprofit sector. For the motion: you meet your own needs and still give. Nonprofit work often disappoints idealists and can turn generosity into frustration and guilt, while a corporate job still lets you volunteer for the rewarding parts. Donations do more good: in a charity you are replaceable, you may spend years doing paperwork, and the best charities are short of cash, not talent. A big donor can also demand reforms, rally wealthy peers and gain political influence.

That was Money versus Happiness. Next episode: Federalization.
