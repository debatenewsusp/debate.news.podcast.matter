Episode 119. Russia.
Today: from the Soviet Union to Putin, the fate of Alexei Navalny, the North Korea partnership, and Prigozhin's mutiny.

First, history. After Lenin, Stalin forced industrialization and collectivization, causing famines that killed millions, and the Great Terror executed hundreds of thousands. Gorbachev's openness, glasnost, and economic restructuring, perestroika, could not save the USSR, which collapsed in 1991 amid nationalism in the republics, stagnation and fragmentation. Russia lost huge territory and population and fell into a deep recession, while voucher privatization of tens of thousands of state firms handed industry to a small group who became the oligarchs.

Second, Putin. A former KGB officer who rose to head the FSB, he was made prime minister in 1999, won popularity with a brutal war in Chechnya, and became president when Yeltsin resigned on the eve of 2000. He struck a bargain with oligarchs: keep your wealth if you stay loyal. Those who did not, like Yukos chief Mikhail Khodorkovsky, who funded pro-democracy causes, were jailed or exiled. Security crises boosted him: after the 2004 Beslan school siege he ended elections for regional governors and expanded police powers. Independent journalists like Anna Politkovskaya were murdered, media came under state control, corruption flourished, and in 2008 he became prime minister while effectively keeping power. Fraud in the 2011 elections sparked mass protests.

Third, Navalny. Starting with a blog in 2006 exposing corruption, Alexei Navalny led the 2011 protests, finished second in Moscow's 2013 mayoral race, built a huge YouTube following and was barred from the presidency on dubious charges. His smart voting strategy, uniting opposition voters behind one candidate, threatened the regime. In 2020 he was poisoned with Novichok, survived, and tricked one of the agents into describing the operation on tape. He returned in 2021, was immediately jailed, and released a film about Putin's secret palace that sparked protests. Since the file was written, he died in an Arctic prison in February 2024.

Fourth, North Korea. In 2023 Kim Jong Un met Putin at a Russian spaceport and pledged support. Russia wanted ammunition compatible with its Soviet-era weapons, and North Korea has vast artillery stocks and many arms factories, possibly in exchange for air defenses or jets. Since then, North Korean troops have fought for Russia against Ukraine's incursion in the Kursk region.

Fifth, Prigozhin. A former convict turned caterer for the Kremlin, Yevgeny Prigozhin also ran troll farms that interfered in the 2016 U.S. election and founded the Wagner Group in 2014, fighting in Crimea, Syria, Libya and across Africa. Wagner relied heavily on convicts and was notoriously brutal, leading the capture of Bakhmut. Prigozhin publicly raged at defense minister Shoigu and chief of staff Gerasimov over incompetence and ammunition shortages. In June 2023 he declared the war's rationale a lie, seized Rostov-on-Don and marched toward Moscow. Putin called it treason, but Belarus's Lukashenko brokered a deal: charges dropped and the march halted. Putin avoided mass arrests, fearing exposure of elite divisions. Two months later, Prigozhin died in a plane crash widely seen as an assassination, and Wagner was absorbed into Russian state control, keeping its lucrative operations in Africa.

That was Russia. Next episode: European Union.
