Episode 69. Fiscal Stimulus.
Today: major economic crises, examples of stimulus, and what causes crises in the first place.

First, the crises. The 2008 financial crisis: a housing bubble built on securitized subprime mortgages burst, Lehman and Bear Stearns collapsed, households lost trillions, and unemployment hit ten percent, prompting the 700 billion dollar bank rescue and Fed bond buying. The 1997 Asian crisis: hot money fueled credit bubbles in Thailand, Korea, Indonesia and Malaysia, pegged currencies became overvalued, speculators attacked, Thailand devalued the baht, and contagion spread. Mexico's 1994 peso crisis: an overvalued peso, dollar debts and political shocks led to devaluation, capital flight, a collapse in the peso and an IMF bailout. Greece from 2010: cheap borrowing after joining the euro fueled deficits, the government revealed its deficit was far higher than reported, and the economy shrank by a quarter with unemployment near twenty-eight percent, requiring three bailouts. Also know the Great Depression, Japan's lost decade after its asset bubble burst, and Latin America's lost decade of debt crises in the eighties.

Second, stimulus. During COVID, governments spent unprecedented sums, including trillions in the U.S. After 2008, the U.S. passed a nearly 800 billion dollar recovery act, China launched a four trillion yuan package, and Australia, Japan and South Korea followed.

Third, what causes crises. Demand shocks reduce willingness to spend and invest, lowering output and prices. Supply shocks reduce the ability to produce, lowering output but raising prices, which is stagflation, and they are harder to fix, because boosting demand fuels inflation.

Demand shocks come from collapsing confidence, as after Lehman's collapse; falling wealth, like Japan's crashing stock and land prices; tighter monetary policy, like Nigeria's 2016 rate hikes; austerity, like Greece; and external shocks like disasters, epidemics and conflict that scare off investment and tourism.

Supply shocks come from volatile commodities, driven by wars, OPEC and disasters like Hurricane Katrina; natural disasters destroying infrastructure; agricultural shocks like droughts, locusts and fertilizer prices tied to energy; and sanctions or strikes disrupting supply chains.

Other explanations: currency crises in emerging markets, where collapsing currencies trigger capital flight and make dollar debts unpayable, as in Mexico, Asia, Russia and Argentina; financial crises, where bad loans freeze credit, captured by Minsky's idea that calm breeds overconfidence until borrowing turns speculative; and the monetarist view, following Milton Friedman, that the money supply and predictable monetary policy matter most.
