Episode 59. Developing World Queer Activism.
Today: Pride events, court victories, and three case studies: Uganda, Sri Lanka and Jamaica.

First, Pride. In 2022 Pride events took place in around a hundred countries, increasingly beyond capital cities, for example in smaller Peruvian and Namibian towns, not just Lima and Windhoek.

Second, courts have driven much of the progress. Costa Rica's Supreme Court struck down its same-sex marriage ban in 2018. India's Supreme Court decriminalized gay sex the same year by striking down the colonial-era Section 377. Colombia's Constitutional Court protected same-sex marriage in 2016. Pakistan and Thailand saw rulings for transgender rights. Botswana's High Court struck down its colonial sodomy law in 2019, Kenya's courts upheld queer people's right to organize in 2023, and Nepal's Supreme Court recognized a third gender back in 2007.

Third, Uganda. President Museveni's Anti-Homosexuality Act criminalized the promotion of homosexuality, gave police sweeping powers and banned the country's leading advocacy group, Sexual Minorities Uganda.

Fourth, Sri Lanka. A British colonial-era penal code still criminalizes homosexuality, though it is rarely enforced and may be repealed. Activists like Rosanna Flamer-Caldera of Equal Ground lead the fight. But groups depend on foreign donors, so Pride often happens near Western embassies and luxury hotels, in English, and mostly in Colombo and Jaffna, which can make the movement look foreign and elite.

Fifth, Jamaica. Laws dating back to 1864 still criminalize gay sex. Homophobia has colonial roots in British legal codes and missionary Christianity, and the story of Sodom and Gomorrah is often cited; the vast majority of Jamaicans say homosexuality is a sin. Some dancehall songs have openly celebrated violence against gay men. The leading group, J-FLAG, lost its leader Brian Williamson in 2004, when he was murdered and some onlookers cheered.
