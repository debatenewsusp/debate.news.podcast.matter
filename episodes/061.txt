Episode 61. Children's Media.
Today: why children's media matters, why it lacks diversity, and why that is changing.

First, examples. Classic books include Where the Wild Things Are, Alice in Wonderland, Pippi Longstocking, The Little Prince, The Hobbit, Narnia, Winnie-the-Pooh, Charlotte's Web and Matilda. Classic films range from The Wizard of Oz and Mary Poppins to My Neighbor Totoro, The Lion King, Toy Story, Frozen and Encanto.

Second, why it matters. Children's stories pass culture between generations, as the Brothers Grimm and Aesop's fables did. They teach values, like Pinocchio teaching not to lie. They entertain, build literacy, and give children emotional tools to face fear and new situations. They offer role models. And they comfort children in hard times: by showing others share their struggles, releasing pent-up feelings, offering escape, teaching coping skills and bringing joy.

Third, the representation problem. Only around four in ten characters on major children's channels are female. Female characters are more often sexualized and solve problems with magic, while male characters use intelligence or strength. Characters of color are more often shown as violent, and disabled or poor characters barely appear. Underrepresentation hurts self-esteem and makes children feel invisible, while good representation breaks stereotypes, as when Mister Rogers shared a foot bath with Officer Clemmons, a Black police officer, at a time of segregated pools.

Why so little diversity? Studios are run mostly by white men. Profit pushes them to appeal to majority audiences and avoid conservative backlash. When they do diversify, they tick several boxes with one character, cast minorities as animals, or recycle stereotypes. And the industry is highly concentrated, so a few executives control the narrative and face little pressure to take risks.

Fourth, reasons for optimism. Films and shows like Coco, Moana, Encanto, Molly of Denali and Craig of the Creek feature Latin American, Polynesian, Indigenous and queer characters, following earlier examples like Sesame Street and Dora the Explorer. Structurally, audiences are becoming more progressive, so diversity sells. Streaming lets companies target niche audiences. And creators skew progressive: they live in cosmopolitan cities, study at liberal arts schools, belong to unions, question the status quo, and their craft of inhabiting other lives builds empathy.
