Episode 17. Social Justice Stock Arguments.
Four parts today: radicalism versus moderation, leaders, wins in court, and the full case for leader-led protests.

First, moderation versus radicalism.

Moderation builds broad coalitions of voters, donors and institutions. Moderate movements get fair media coverage, seats at the negotiating table, are harder to demonize or crack down on, and split less.

Radicalism shifts the Overton window, increases bargaining power, grabs media attention and energizes the most committed supporters, who more than make up for lost moderates. Radical tactics can make defending the status quo too costly. Small, radical movements also resist commercialization, co-optation by elites, slacktivism and burnout.

Second, leaders. Against leaders: they tend to come from privileged backgrounds and may sideline intersectional concerns, and relying on them is risky, since they can be co-opted, discredited or killed. For leaders: they keep movements going through setbacks, act as credible go-betweens with governments, and hold coalitions together.

Third, court victories. Many advances for same-sex marriage came through courts: South Africa in 2006, Taiwan in 2017, Costa Rica in 2018, Ecuador and Austria in 2019, Obergefell in the U.S. in 2015, and India's 2018 ruling decriminalizing gay sex.

Fourth, the full case for leader-led movements. Start with who leaders are: they rise organically by earning trust, must keep that trust or face backlash, and are usually deeply committed.

Argument one: leaders sustain movements. Most movements fail at first and supporters lose faith. Leaders inspire people to keep going and fight burnout, the way Mandela's courage carried anti-apartheid activists. Leaders also prevent fragmentation by mediating disputes over strategy, and they make breakaway factions look futile.

Argument two: leaders pressure authorities effectively. Leaderless movements scatter limited resources; leaders can focus them on a few decisive fights. Leaders also solve the collective action problem: you only join a risky protest if you trust many others will, and a recognized leader's call makes that credible. That is why strikes rarely work without union leadership.

Argument three: leaders make negotiation possible. Governments only concede if they get something back, like an end to protests or boycotts. Nobody in a leaderless movement can credibly promise that. Governments also fear that conceding to leaderless protests will encourage escalation. A leader can promise de-escalation and be believed.

Argument four: leaderless movements are easier to demonize. With no single message, media coverage focuses on the most radical wing, alienating moderates and justifying crackdowns. Leaders shape the narrative, as Martin Luther King did for civil rights, and restrain violent fringes, which would otherwise discredit the movement and invite repression.
