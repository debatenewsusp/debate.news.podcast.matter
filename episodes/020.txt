Episode 20. State Power Principle.
Today: the State Power Principle, how to refute it, autonomy, arguments against democracy, and the social contract.

First, the State Power Principle, a classic case for why democracy is an absolute right. Premise one: the state restricts our freedom, taxing us and imprisoning us under threat of force. Its benefits, like roads, do not justify this, because a benefit you cannot refuse does not license taking payment for it. Premise two: we never consented. Nobody chose where they were born, and there is nowhere to opt out to. Premise three: so the state owes us reparation, and the right way to repay us is to let us shape the laws imposed on us, especially since people disagree about what the good life is.

Now, refuting it, one premise at a time.

Against premise one: poverty and violence restrict freedom far more than the state does. And the state actually increases freedom, because unlimited liberty is self-defeating. Without law, people take from each other by force, and fear keeps everyone at home. Limiting everyone a little makes everyone freer overall, so there is nothing to repay.

Against premise two: you could, in theory, live in barely governed mountains, or vote for anarchist parties. Choosing not to is implicit consent. And if your vote is too powerless to matter, democracy is a weak form of reparation anyway. There is also hypothetical consent: if I tackle you before you step onto a collapsing bridge, you would have agreed had you known. Any rational person would choose the state over a state of nature that is nasty, brutish and short.

Against premise three: the state already repays us through security, property rights and public goods. If liberty matters because it makes lives go better, that is a utilitarian case, so where voters do not understand an issue, like central banking, outcomes should win. Some argue free will barely exists, given determinism and the brain's chemistry, so liberty carries less weight than pleasure and pain. And reparation should go first to those most constrained, like minorities, future generations and even animals, rather than to the majority.

Next, autonomy. Things only have value because we assign it, so the freedom to choose matters more than raw happiness. A machine flooding your brain with pleasure would make life meaningless. And individuals know their own preferences best.

Next, principles against democracy. Democracy is instrumentally useful, but your single vote never matters, and real democracies suffer from corruption, lobbying, gerrymandering and capture. It must be weighed against rights like food and shelter. This logic supports giving the poor extra votes: the state has historically harmed them, wealth buys political power, and they depend most on state policy.

Finally, the social contract does not exist. Nobody signed it, and agreeing under the threat of the state of nature is like signing a contract while drowning. Staying is not consent, because emigrating is costly, there is no empty land, and choosing between states is choosing the lesser evil. Voting is not consent either, since many abstain or lose. And hypothetical consent cannot justify taking your car just because a reasonable person might have sold it.

That was State Power Principle. Next episode: Money versus Happiness.
