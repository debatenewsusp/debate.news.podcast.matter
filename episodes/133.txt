Episode 133. Saudi Arabia.
Today: Saudi human rights, the U.S.-Saudi relationship, Vision 2030 and sportswashing, with two motions on sovereign wealth funds and golf.

First, the charges against Riyadh. The death penalty is used widely, including a mass execution of eighty-one people in March 2022. A special criminal court targets dissidents and religious minorities; torture, coerced confessions and arbitrary detention are common; migrant workers suffer under the kafala sponsorship system; and women and LGBTQ people remain heavily repressed. On 9/11, fifteen of the nineteen hijackers were Saudi, and there are allegations that Saudi officials helped some of them. Riyadh also spent tens of billions spreading puritanical Wahhabi teaching abroad. In 2018, journalist Jamal Khashoggi, a critic of Crown Prince Mohammed bin Salman, was murdered and dismembered by Saudi agents inside the consulate in Istanbul, with strong evidence the order came from Riyadh. And since 2015, a Saudi-led coalition has bombed Iran-backed Houthi rebels in Yemen and blockaded its ports, contributing to famine, largely with American weapons.

Second, why the U.S. stays close. Counterterrorism: after al-Qaeda attacks in Riyadh in 2003, the Saudis began sharing intelligence against al-Qaeda and later the Islamic State, though suspicions of covert support for some groups persist. Arms: ties date to Roosevelt's meeting with King Abdulaziz in 1945; U.S. troops defended the kingdom in the 1991 Gulf War, a presence that fueled al-Qaeda's grievances; and Saudi Arabia is the largest buyer of U.S. weapons. Oil: Saudi Arabia holds about a sixth of the world's proven reserves, Aramco is the world's most profitable oil company, and Riyadh leads OPEC and helps keep oil priced in dollars, though OPEC production cuts often frustrate Washington. Friction exists too: Riyadh opposed the 2003 Iraq invasion and the Iran nuclear deal.

Third, Vision 2030. The Public Investment Fund, a sovereign wealth fund of over six hundred billion dollars effectively run by the crown prince, is diversifying away from oil: the futuristic city Neom, a new airline and airport, stakes in gaming, finance and tech firms. Society has opened: women can drive and travel without male permission, concerts and cinemas are allowed, and the religious police lost power. Yet in 2017, dozens of princes and businessmen were detained in the Ritz-Carlton and stripped of billions. With most Saudis under thirty, the state has cut subsidies, pushed people into private jobs, introduced VAT and sold a slice of Aramco.

Fourth, sportswashing: WWE events, boxing, Formula One, the purchase of Newcastle United, and Cristiano Ronaldo's move to Al Nassr.

Motion: This House supports the global rise of sovereign wealth funds. Government: they turn temporary windfalls from finite resources into long-term returns, cushion commodity shocks and stabilize currencies, bring know-how and patient capital to developing markets, support long-term governance, and buy during downturns, steadying markets. Opposition: they are political tools prone to patronage and corruption, opaque and destabilizing, can provoke protectionism, can be forced into fire sales in crises, and funds of authoritarian states can threaten national security.

Fifth, LIV Golf. Launched in 2022 with Saudi money, it offered huge guaranteed contracts, a shorter fifty-four-hole format, team play and music, luring stars like Phil Mickelson and Dustin Johnson. The PGA Tour banned defectors and faced antitrust suits, then surprisingly announced a merger in June 2023.

Motion: This House regrets the PGA-LIV merger. Government: it is a major sportswashing win and teaches Riyadh that money can bend whole industries. Opposition: the scandal spotlights Saudi abuses, and without a deal, lawsuits and bidding wars might give the fund even more power over golf.

Since the file was written: the merger was never finalized, Saudi Arabia was confirmed as host of the 2034 World Cup, and under Trump's second term ties deepened with huge arms and investment deals.

That was Saudi Arabia. Next episode: Japan.
