Episode 25. Standardized Tests.
Today: are standardized tests good or bad metrics, and are they biased or fair?

First, tests are bad metrics. They cover a narrow slice of ability, like grammar and algebra, and miss creativity. They capture a few hours on one day, when a student may be sick or stressed by a crisis at home, unlike grades that span years. Exam anxiety makes some students freeze. And being a good test-taker says little about the work ethic and study habits that drive success in college.

Second, tests are good metrics. They are the same for everyone, while grades vary between teachers and schools. And testing companies have every incentive to make the tests informative, because colleges would stop using them otherwise.

Third, tests are biased. Wealthy families pay for tutors and prep, and some even game disability accommodations for extra time. Richer schools have better teachers and curricula focused on the test. Poorer students often work or care for siblings after school, leaving less time to prepare, and the tests mostly reward knowing the test. They also enforce one standard form of language, penalizing students who speak differently. Minority groups score lower on average.

Fourth, tests are fair. They are objective, so imbalances are easier to spot and correct, and they leave less room for the hidden biases of admissions officers reading essays and recommendation letters. Everyone knows what material is covered. A high score lets a talented student from an underfunded school stand out when the rest of their application cannot compete with wealthy applicants. And free prep resources, volunteer tutors and fee waivers are increasingly available.
