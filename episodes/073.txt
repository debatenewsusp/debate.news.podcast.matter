Episode 73. Islamic Feminism.
Today: what Islamic feminism is, key figures, a Libyan case study, the veiling debate, Iran, and the case for and against.

First, foundations. Islamic feminism reconciles the Quran and hadith with gender equality by reinterpreting texts to bring out their egalitarian values. Key scholars include Amina Wadud, Leila Ahmed and Fatema Mernissi. Activists include Zainah Anwar of Malaysia's Sisters in Islam, Egypt's Nawal El Saadawi, who campaigned against female genital mutilation, and Iran's Shirin Ebadi, winner of the 2003 Nobel Peace Prize. Movements include Musawah and Pakistan's annual Aurat March.

Second, a case study. Voice of Libyan Women's Project Noor tackled domestic violence with a national media campaign built on Islamic messages, like the saying of Aisha, the Prophet's wife, that he never hit a woman, rather than Western health statistics. It sparked conversations in schools, cafés and mosques and undercut false religious justifications for abuse.

Third, veiling. Surah 24 of the Quran tells believing women to guard their modesty and draw their coverings over their chests. By the early sixties, veiling had largely faded in much of the Arab world, but after Israel's 1967 victory shattered secular pan-Arabism, Islamist movements rose and Saudi Arabia spread Wahhabism, and veiling returned. Many women find empowerment in it: piety, connection to faith, pride in identity in the face of discrimination, and rejection of Western stereotypes. Critics argue that where it is socially or legally mandatory it removes choice, turns women into symbols of their community and amounts to gendered erasure.

Fourth, Iran. After the 1979 revolution, the regime repealed family protection laws, imposed dress codes and restricted contraception. In the nineties, partial reform followed, driven by economic need, reformist thinkers, women in parliament and leaders like Rafsanjani.

Fifth, the case for Islamic feminism. Scholar Afsaneh Najmabadi argued it removes clerics' monopoly on interpretation and lets women interpret scripture. It is homegrown, rooted in local values rather than seen as a Western import, and it rejects the patronizing idea that Muslim women need saving. It argues that patriarchy comes from pre-Islamic custom, not Islam, and that reading the Quran verse by verse lost its egalitarian themes, while core beliefs like God's justice and uniqueness reject male supremacy.

Sixth, the case against. Reinterpretation is futile: clerics dismiss feminists as unqualified, conservative readings are entrenched in law and custom, and activists are squeezed between charges of Westernization and apologism. Some argue Islam itself is incompatible with full equality, especially on sexuality, so Islamic feminism ends up justifying subordination like veiling. It is often led by Western-educated elites far from local realities, may legitimize Islamist regimes, and divides feminists from the secular left.
