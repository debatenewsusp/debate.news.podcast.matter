Episode 139. Vietnam.
Today: two thousand years of resisting foreign rule, the wars of the twentieth century, Vietnam's economic rise, and its bamboo diplomacy between China and the U.S.

First, the long struggle with China. Early cultures like the Dong Son, famous for bronze drums, grew rice in the north, while the Cham lived in the south. In 111 BCE, China's Han dynasty conquered the region, beginning roughly a thousand years of Chinese domination, with forced sinicization: Confucian bureaucracy, imperial exams and patriarchal marriage laws. Resistance became the core of Vietnamese identity. In 40 CE, the Trung sisters led a largely female army that expelled the Chinese governor, before being crushed two years later; they remain national heroines. Lady Trieu, often called the Vietnamese Joan of Arc, led another revolt in the third century. Independence finally came in 938, when Ngo Quyen planted iron-tipped stakes in the Bach Dang River at low tide and trapped the Southern Han fleet. The Tran dynasty later repelled three Mongol invasions in the thirteenth century. China's Ming dynasty occupied Vietnam again from 1407, burning its literature, until Le Loi's guerrilla uprising drove them out in 1427.

Second, expansion and division. Vietnam pushed south, absorbing the Cham, but split between rival clans for centuries. The Nguyen dynasty reunified the country in 1802 and named it Viet Nam. Emperor Minh Mang cut ties with Europe, persecuted Catholics and other minorities, and imposed Vietnamese culture on Cambodia, provoking revolts and wars with Siam.

Third, colonialism. France conquered Vietnam between 1858 and 1885, later forming French Indochina with Cambodia and Laos. Exploitation and repression fueled nationalist and communist movements. In World War Two, Japan occupied Indochina, and a famine in 1945 killed up to a million people. After Japan's defeat, Ho Chi Minh's Viet Minh declared independence in September 1945.

Fourth, the wars. France returned, and the First Indochina War ended with the Viet Minh's victory at Dien Bien Phu in 1954. The Geneva Conference split the country at the seventeenth parallel between a communist North and a U.S.-backed South under Ngo Dinh Diem. Citing the domino theory, Kennedy expanded U.S. involvement, and after the 1964 Gulf of Tonkin incident, Johnson bombed the North and sent hundreds of thousands of troops, fighting a war of attrition while supplies flowed down the Ho Chi Minh Trail. In the 1968 Tet Offensive, communist forces attacked over a hundred towns. It was a military failure, devastating the Viet Cong, but a political victory: televised images and broken promises of progress turned the American public against the war. The U.S. left after the 1973 Paris Peace Accords, and Saigon fell in 1975. Vietnam then invaded Cambodia in 1978, fought a brief border war with China, normalized ties with the U.S. in 1995 and joined the WTO in 2007.

Fifth, politics. The Communist Party rules unchallenged, with no tolerance for dissent. Its long-time leader Nguyen Phu Trong, a conservative Marxist-Leninist, led a sweeping anti-corruption campaign, which critics say targets high-profile cases for legitimacy rather than everyday graft.

Sixth, the economy. After 1975, central planning failed. The 1986 Doi Moi reforms created a socialist-oriented market economy, and income per person rose from about four hundred dollars to over four thousand. Vietnam signed trade deals with the U.S., the EU, Britain, and joined the CPTPP and RCEP. As companies diversify away from China, Apple suppliers, Samsung, Lego and chipmakers like Intel and Amkor have expanded there, drawn by its location, educated workforce and rare earth reserves. Weaknesses include low productivity, little value added, power outages and a plan that still relies heavily on coal. A property crisis hit in 2022: fearing a China-style debt bomb after Evergrande, regulators tightened credit, arrests of tycoons like Truong My Lan sparked panic and a bank run, rate hikes defended the dong's peg, and bond rules tightened, so hundreds of developers collapsed. Optimists note housing matters far less to Vietnam's economy than to China's, and the middle class is still growing.

Seventh, bamboo diplomacy. Vietnam follows the four nos: no military alliances, no siding with one country against another, no foreign bases, no use of force. Tensions with China over the South China Sea, from a Chinese oil rig in 2014 to island-building, have made Hanoi wary of Chinese infrastructure. In September 2023, Biden's visit raised ties to a comprehensive strategic partnership, the top tier, alongside China and Russia. Yet weeks later, Xi Jinping visited, and the two sides signed security agreements including a South China Sea hotline. Like bamboo, Hanoi bends with the wind but stays rooted.

Since the file was written: Nguyen Phu Trong died in 2024 and was succeeded by To Lam, who launched a sweeping overhaul of the state, and Truong My Lan was sentenced to death in the country's largest fraud case.
