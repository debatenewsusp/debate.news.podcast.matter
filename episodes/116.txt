Episode 116. Syria.
Today: Syria under the Assads, the Arab Spring uprising, the civil war, the foreign powers involved, and the humanitarian toll.

First, the Assads. Hafez al-Assad, from the Alawite minority, took power in 1970 and ruled brutally. In 1982, when the Muslim Brotherhood rose up in the city of Hama, his army killed well over twenty-five thousand people, a lesson his son Bashar later followed: meet protest with maximum force. Hafez built a kleptocracy of cronies and corrupt state firms. Bashar took over in 2000 promising reform, privatized and cut subsidies and price controls, which deepened rural poverty, and a severe drought from 2006 drove ruined farmers into slums around the cities.

Second, the uprising. After a Tunisian vendor's self-immolation sparked the Arab Spring in late 2010, children in Deraa painted anti-regime graffiti in 2011 and were arrested and tortured. Protests spread demanding reform. Assad offered token concessions while preparing a crackdown: troops fired on protesters, thousands were arrested or executed, and tanks moved in. He framed the opposition as Sunni extremists.

Third, the war. Defectors formed the Free Syrian Army, but it was divided and outgunned, and many fighters drifted to more successful Islamist groups like the al-Nusra Front, al-Qaeda's Syrian branch, and later ISIS, which seized huge territory with shocking brutality. Assad even released jihadists from prison so he could present himself as the only alternative to terrorists.

Fourth, the foreign powers. Russia backed Assad with weapons, airstrikes and UN vetoes, in exchange for its naval base at Tartus and an air base in Latakia. Iran poured in billions, for strategic rather than religious reasons: rivalry with Saudi Arabia and a supply corridor to Hezbollah. Turkey backed rebels but focused on fighting Kurdish militias, invading northern Syria in 2019. Saudi Arabia funded Sunni rebels to counter Iran. The U.S. and allies bombed ISIS and backed the Kurdish-led Syrian Democratic Forces. Obama was criticized for not enforcing his 2013 red line on chemical weapons; Trump struck regime bases in 2017 after a chemical attack, then withdrew most troops in 2019, opening the door to Turkey.

Fifth, the human cost: hundreds of thousands dead, over six million displaced inside Syria and another six million refugees, mostly in Turkey, Lebanon and Jordan. In 2015, over a million people reached Europe, the EU paid Turkey to hold back migrants, and the crisis fueled right-wing populism across Europe.

Since the file was written: in December 2024, a rapid rebel offensive led by the Islamist group Hayat Tahrir al-Sham toppled Assad, who fled to Russia, and its leader Ahmed al-Sharaa became Syria's interim president.

That was Syria. Next episode: Afghanistan.
