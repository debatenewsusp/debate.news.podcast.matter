Episode 48. Populist Politics.
Today: what populism is, its six features, where it rules, and key European populist parties.

First, three types: cultural populism, built on nationalism and hostility to outsiders; economic populism, defending the hardworking middle class against big business and globalization; and anti-establishment populism, against political elites.

Second, six features. One, they claim to be the people, as in Chávez's slogan that he was the people, and say nothing should block the popular will. Two, they reject elitism and political correctness, use crude language and celebrate the common man, like Trump or Farage. Three, they divide society into insiders and outsiders, targeting experts, oligarchs, cosmopolitans, migrants and minorities. They usually exploit existing tensions and institutional decay rather than create them. Four, they weaponize anger, linking isolated failures to broad anxieties and reviving issues mainstream parties avoid, like trade or the EU. Five, they embrace strongman politics, attacking courts, media and civil society as obstacles to the people. That is the paradox: populism undermines democracy in the name of democracy, as when Chávez swore over what he called a dying constitution. Six, personalism: Chávez cast himself as the new Bolívar, the Kirchners as the new Peróns, and leaders like Modi speak directly to citizens to bypass the media.

Third, reach. Populism has grown roughly fourfold since 1990, with leaders like Trump, Bolsonaro, Erdogan, Modi, Duterte, Orbán and Maduro. Latin America has a long history, from the Pink Tide of Chávez, Morales, Lula and Correa to newer leftist leaders like Boric and López Obrador.

Fourth, European parties, as of the file's writing in 2023. Poland's Law and Justice: soft Euroscepticism, state economic intervention, social conservatism, and democratic backsliding through weaker courts and captured public media. France's National Rally under Marine Le Pen: anti-immigration, protectionist, critical of the EU and of sanctions on Russia. Brothers of Italy under Giorgia Meloni, with roots in post-fascism: anti-immigration, pro-natalist and focused on family and Christian values. The Sweden Democrats: originally tied to neo-Nazi circles, since moderated, and backing the center-right government. Greece's Syriza: left-wing, anti-austerity, which governed from 2015 to 2019 but stepped back from leaving the euro. And Austria's Freedom Party: nationalist, opposed to Turkish EU membership, with historical ties to neo-Nazism.
