Episode 1. State Ownership.
Two sides today: the case for nationalization, and the case for privatization.

First, nationalization.

The core claim is that private profit incentives raise prices, cut quality and squeeze workers, while governments face political pressure to keep services affordable.

Start with natural monopolies. Markets only discipline firms when new competitors can enter. When barriers to entry are huge, think economies of scale, network effects, patents or exclusive distribution deals, competition disappears. If demand is also inelastic, like water or electricity, companies can price gouge, and workers face a monopsony with nowhere else to go. In those sectors, the state should own the firm.

Second, incentives. Governments must appeal to a broad electorate, report publicly and can be sued for failing their legal duties. Corporations answer only to shareholders. And voters believe they can change a government, but almost nobody believes they can change a multinational.

Third, time horizons. Listed companies chase quarterly earnings. Governments care about legacy, and long-term projects like infrastructure create short-term jobs that voters reward. State-owned firms also keep profits at home and can cross-subsidize services in poor areas where no private firm would go.

Finally, why not just regulate? Because regulation fails. Lobbying guts rules, armies of lawyers find loopholes, regulators lag behind fast-moving firms, and agencies get captured through the revolving door. Privatization itself is often corrupt, with billion-dollar sales negotiated behind closed doors.

Now, privatization.

The core claim flips: state-owned enterprises become vehicles for corruption and patronage, and they deliver poor service.

First, efficiency. SOEs are shielded from competition, so waste goes unpunished. Soft budget constraints create zombie firms that need bailout after bailout, piling up public debt. Bureaucrats lack technical expertise, and managers rotate constantly as political appointees come and go. Private firms face the constant threat of death by competition, and they borrow more cheaply than cash-strapped governments.

Second, corruption. SOE jobs are handed out as rewards to loyal cronies, and the managers then gain leverage over politicians, lobbying for protection, cheap loans and bailouts. SOE revenues also reduce the state's need for taxes, weakening voters and funding the security apparatus, which hurts democratization.

Third, long-termism actually favors companies. Big firms are owned by pension funds with decades-long horizons, executives are paid in long-dated stock, and anyone who spends billions buying a privatized firm has a revealed preference for long-term profit. Governments, by contrast, raid SOE profits for pre-election handouts.

Fourth, markets. Nationalization scares investors and triggers capital flight, while privatization signals fiscal discipline and raises cash for the state. SOEs also distort credit markets, because implicit state guarantees let them hoard cheap loans and crowd out private firms.

Last, democratic accountability is weak in practice. Voters rarely swing elections over SOE management, and blame gets diffused. The better answer is targeted regulation, like price caps, which tackles the specific harm without handing an industry to the state.
