Episode 32. Transnational Criminal Organizations.
Today: two case studies in organized crime, MS-13 and the Sinaloa Cartel.

First, the landscape. Major groups include Colombia's ELN and ex-FARC networks, Brazil's PCC, the street gangs MS-13 and 18th Street, and Mexican cartels like Sinaloa, Jalisco New Generation, the Gulf Cartel and Los Zetas.

Second, MS-13. It began in the 1980s among Salvadoran immigrants in Los Angeles seeking protection, then spread back to Central America when members were deported after El Salvador's civil war. It now operates across El Salvador, Honduras, Guatemala, the United States and even Europe. It has no clear leader, just loose local cliques. What holds it together is community and hatred of its rival, 18th Street: members are beaten in during initiation, must kill to prove loyalty, and are even banned from saying the number eighteen. It barely needs to recruit, because poverty, addiction, deportation and prison push young men toward it. Violence, often with machetes, is its brand: it protects territory and extortion income, deters rivals and police, punishes informants and gives the gang leverage to bargain with the state. A secret 2012 truce with 18th Street, brokered with Church help in El Salvador, collapsed by 2014 after public outrage. Its money comes from extortion, drugs and front businesses.

Third, the Sinaloa Cartel, perhaps the world's most powerful, controlling a large share of Mexico's drug trade in cocaine, heroin, meth and fentanyl. It emerged from the collapse of the Guadalajara Cartel in the late eighties, when El Chapo Guzmán and partners built smuggling routes into the U.S. using tunnels, boats, planes and bribed border officials. El Chapo escaped prison twice before his recapture in 2016 and extradition, and his sons, known as Los Chapitos, have since fought El Mayo Zambada for control. Why is it so durable? A decentralized structure that survives the loss of leaders, family ties that ensure loyalty, its role as a shadow government providing security and dispute resolution, deep bribery of police and politicians, alliances with other cartels, and strategic rather than impulsive violence.
