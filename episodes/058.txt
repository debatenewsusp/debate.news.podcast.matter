Episode 58. Judicial System.
Today: legal education and diversity, judicial legitimacy, judicial elections, and plea bargaining.

First, legal education. Law schools socialize students into the profession's norms and teach theory, but often leave graduates unprepared for practice. They are also stressful, with heavy debt and intense competition, and high rates of mental illness and substance abuse.

Second, diversity. Very few U.S. Supreme Court justices have not been white men. Women and minorities were long excluded from law schools; Harvard Law admitted no women until 1950. Elite schools dominate clerkships and top jobs, top firms cluster in big cities where rural students lack connections, and partners remain overwhelmingly white and male. Diversity matters for public trust, role models, and better policy shaped by different lived experiences.

Third, legitimacy. Courts have neither the purse nor the sword, only judgment, as Hamilton wrote, so they depend on public faith that they stand above politics. Once judges look like politicians in robes, they face attacks, court packing, impeachment threats and limits on their power.

Fourth, judicial elections. Most U.S. states elect at least some judges, yet most voters do not know the candidates.

Against them: judges must be impartial and stay out of politics, unlike legislators. Campaigns bring mudslinging and misinformation. Voters lack information and decide on names or appearance. Campaign money breeds corruption, or at least its appearance. Races turn into fights over hot-button issues like crime and abortion, low-turnout primaries reward extremists, and judges who rule correctly but unpopularly, say by freeing someone on a technicality, get voted out, which pressures colleagues. That is the crocodile in the bathtub.

For them: most races are quiet and incumbents usually win. Campaigning connects judges with ordinary people and demystifies them. Elections offer accountability where impeachment is slow and peer discipline weak. Appointments are corrupted by patronage anyway. And since judges effectively make law, those subject to it should choose them.

Fifth, plea bargaining.

Against it: it hands power to prosecutors who effectively convict people behind closed doors. They pressure defendants, even innocent ones, with extra charges, harsh sentence recommendations and pretrial detention, backed by mandatory minimums, so accepting a lesser sentence becomes rational even for the innocent. As more innocent people plead, acquittal rates fall, which convinces the next defendant that trial is hopeless. Negotiations lack courtroom safeguards. Prosecutors want lighter workloads and better win records, overworked public defenders face pressure from judges to close cases quickly, and defendants know far less than prosecutors. It can leave victims feeling justice was not done, deepens racial inequities, and erodes trust in courts.

For it: courts are overloaded and plea deals keep justice moving, freeing resources for hard cases. Both sides have a right to a mutually beneficial deal. Many victims want closure, fear testifying or fear acquittal, and value an admission of guilt. Banning it would drive deals underground. The better answer is reform: mandatory disclosure of evidence, ending mandatory minimums, stronger checks that consent is informed, and judicial oversight of negotiations.
