Episode 129. Françafrique.
Today: France's sphere of influence in Africa, the CFA franc debate, and why French power is fading.

First, origins. After World War Two and defeats in Indochina and Algeria, de Gaulle sought to preserve French grandeur. Françafrique kept influence over former colonies through economics, like the CFA franc and trade deals, and security, like military bases, training and interventions, all held together by close, family-like ties between French and African elites.

Second, the CFA franc. France imposed it on its former colonies, and when Guinea refused, French intelligence flooded it with forged Guinean notes to cause hyperinflation. Critics say it is monetary colonialism: reserves are held in France, the peg to the euro hurts African exports, and countries cannot devalue or set their own policy. Defenders say it brings stability: inflation has averaged about six percent in Ivory Coast, which uses the franc, against about forty percent in neighboring Ghana; it eases regional trade and attracts investment by removing exchange rate risk. A middle path: peg to a basket including the dollar and yuan and loosen reserve rules.

Third, France's interests: limiting migration to Europe, fighting jihadist groups like Boko Haram and al-Qaeda's affiliates, resources like Niger's uranium and North African oil and gas through TotalEnergies, and prestige that helps justify its UN Security Council seat. Since 2017, a vigilance law requires large French firms to prevent human rights and environmental abuses along their operations.

Fourth, the backlash. After France withdrew from the Central African Republic in 2016, Wagner protected its president in exchange for gold and timber. In Mali, Burkina Faso and Niger, juntas that seized power between 2021 and 2023 expelled French troops and turned to Russia. This costs France bases, intelligence and influence over migration, while juntas control the media and spread anti-French narratives. Critics add that ad hoc military interventions were bound to fail without development and strong institutions. Chinese oil firms, more tolerant of risk, now compete too.

Fifth, why the decline? The end of the Cold War removed France's role as a balancing force; joining deeper EU structures made opaque ties with dictators harder; French voters tired of the costs; key figures of the network died; and scandals, above all France's role in the 1994 Rwandan genocide, discredited it. In 1994 France also devalued the CFA franc by half, sparking riots, and shifted toward multilateral, IMF-style conditions and joint operations with the U.S. and Britain. Still, skeptics note France has intervened in about twenty-five African states since the nineties.

Since the file was written: French troops have also left Chad, Senegal and Ivory Coast, shrinking its permanent presence to a handful of bases.

That was Françafrique. Next episode: DRC.
