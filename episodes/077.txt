Episode 77. Religious Conflict Isn't Religious.
Today: the argument that religion is rarely the real cause of war, with historical examples and alternative theories of conflict.

First, the claim. Religion is a tool and a smokescreen for violence more than its cause. One encyclopedia of wars found only about seven percent of nearly eighteen hundred historical conflicts were primarily religious. Sunni-Shia tensions today reflect nationalism and regional rivalries more than a split dating back to 632. The Troubles in Northern Ireland were about unionism versus nationalism. France's sixteenth-century Wars of Religion were really power struggles among noble houses. Sri Lanka's civil war was ethnic, inflamed by colonial rule. And Europe's wars after the Reformation, including the Thirty Years' War, were driven by dynastic rivalry, geopolitics, poverty and local separatism.

Second, war was never really religious. For most of history, religion was not a private belief but an all-encompassing way of life fused with the state, and states were violent. Many ancient languages had no separate word for religion; Arabic din and Sanskrit dharma also mean law, politics and order. So calling old wars religious confuses correlation with cause.

Third, the Crusades. Pope Urban II's 1095 call was framed as defending the Byzantine Empire against the Seljuk Turks, not a war on Islam. Crusader charters focused on crimes against Eastern Christians, many participants were after land and wealth, as in the massacres in the Rhineland, and leaders like Baldwin and Godfrey were chasing personal power and principalities.

Fourth, alternative explanations. Evolutionary: humans evolved in tribes, loyal to insiders and hostile to outsiders, and research suggests we have an unusually high capacity for planned, goal-driven aggression. Sociological: groups fight over scarce resources, and the larger an outgroup, the bigger the perceived threat. Ethno-nationalist: people identify with ethnicity, nation or caste, and religion may amplify these identities but the grievances are not religious. Socioeconomic: Marx saw conflict in unequal power and resources; poverty and inequality anger the deprived, push the privileged to defend themselves, lower the cost of joining a rebellion, and give elites discontent to exploit, while Paul Collier's greed hypothesis says people fight for material gain.
