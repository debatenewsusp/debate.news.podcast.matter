Episode 109. PLA Invasion of Taiwan.
Today: why China wants Taiwan, America's commitments and interests, Taiwan's defenses, and how an invasion might unfold.

First, China's motives. Ideologically, taking Taiwan would end the civil war and complete recovery from the Century of Humiliation; the 2005 Anti-Secession Law authorizes force. Strategically, Taiwan is an unsinkable aircraft carrier, and Beijing distrusts its ties with the West.

Second, America's position. The 1979 Taiwan Relations Act created unofficial ties and requires the U.S. to provide defensive weapons, but does not commit it to defend Taiwan, a policy of strategic ambiguity, though Biden said several times the U.S. would defend it. U.S. interests: credibility with allies like Japan, Korea and the Philippines; shared democratic values; TSMC, which makes over ninety percent of the most advanced chips, the so-called silicon shield; and fear that backing down would embolden Beijing everywhere. Yet the U.S. also opposes a formal declaration of independence, deterring both sides. Ties deepened under Trump's call with Tsai Ing-wen and big arms sales, and under Biden, with Nancy Pelosi's 2022 visit.

Third, Chinese pressure: hundreds of military flights into Taiwan's air defense zone each year, warships in the strait, constant cyberattacks, punishment of countries like Lithuania for engaging Taiwan, and election interference.

Fourth, Taiwan's military. Reasons for concern: defense spending was only around two percent of GDP, many units are understaffed, conscription was only recently extended to a year, training relies on outdated weapons and tactics, pay is low, and the military still buys expensive ships and jets that Chinese missiles could quickly destroy instead of fully embracing a porcupine strategy of cheap, mobile defenses. U.S. aid is slowed by backlogs and strained by Ukraine. Reasons for optimism: budgets and conscript pay are rising, most Taiwanese reject unification and say they would fight, civil defense groups are training volunteers, a Taiwanese identity has grown sharply, and decades of U.S. arms and training help.

Fifth, an invasion. China would likely start with a blockade or bombardment to isolate the island, then missile strikes on ports, airfields and ships. It would then need to ferry troops across a strait about a hundred and thirty to a hundred and eighty kilometers wide, using amphibious ships and civilian ferries, under fire from anti-ship missiles, submarines and mines, and in typhoon-prone weather. Then it must seize a beachhead and a working port or airfield, against entrenched defenders who might sabotage them, and hold off U.S. forces. Inland, mudflats, mountains and forests favor defenders, and Taiwan's military could retreat east and fight a long guerrilla war. Leaked U.S. wargames suggested trouble for America's side, but a major CSIS study of two dozen scenarios found China usually fails, at enormous cost to everyone, and only wins when Taiwan stands alone.

Finally, the wider war. The U.S. has bases across Japan, the Philippines, South Korea and Guam. Striking mainland China would risk nuclear escalation. Japan, since its 2015 security laws, can join collective self-defense, and North Korea might exploit the moment.
